Ukrainian Artillery and Fire Support:
Open-Source Gap Analysis, Training,
Evidentiary Audit, and Platform
Specifications

This reference supplement fills seven standing functional gaps in Ukrainian artillery and
fire-support documentation (Task A), records Ukraine's multinational and domestic artillery
training pipelines (Task B), audits the evidentiary basis of the existing entries "What the Artillery
War Teaches" and "Precision, Mines and Civilian Harm" (Task C), compiles technical
specifications for the AHS Krab, M777, and M120 Rak (Task D), and concludes with a URL-level
source verification log and audit of existing entries (Final Step). Open-source evidence spans
pre-2022 baselines through November 2026, drawing primarily on official publications from the
Ministry of Defence of Ukraine, the Council of the European Union, the European External
Action Service (EEAS), the U.S. Department of Defense, the UK Ministry of Defence, and the
U.S. Government Accountability Office (GAO), alongside established research from the Royal
United Services Institute (RUSI), the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS), the
International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS), and Human Rights Watch (HRW).
Four operational takeaways anchor this update. First, Ukrainian fire support diverges sharply
from Western forward-observer doctrine: units generally do not embed fire controllers inside
maneuver companies, instead streaming continuous multicopter reconnaissance feeds into
command posts that deconflict and authorize fires across Contact, Middle, and Deep Battle
Areas. Second, while tactical uncrewed aerial vehicles (UAVs) accounted for an estimated
60–70% of damaged and destroyed Russian systems by early 2025, 60–80% of first-person
view (FPV) drones fail to reach their target, leaving howitzers and mortars irreplaceable for
low-latency, all-weather, electronic-warfare-resilient high-explosive effects. Third, Ukraine has
begun offsetting steep U.S. supply dependencies—estimated by the Kiel Institute via CSIS at
86% for rocket artillery and 82% for howitzer ammunition as of March 2025—by scaling
domestic 152 mm and 155 mm shell output and conducting sustained land-attack cruise missile
campaigns with R-360L Long Neptune systems in late 2026. Fourth, several narrow technical
topics—Ukrainian ballistic calibration of mixed donor propellant lots, operational logs of COBRA
and ARTHUR counter-battery radars, primary Izium anti-personnel mine reports, Lithuanian
Panzerhaubitze 2000 depot records, and manufacturer-published displacement times for the
AHS Krab—lack primary documentation in available open sources and are explicitly marked as
standing collection gaps rather than extrapolated.

Task A: Gap-Filling Reference Entries

Liaison and Fire Support Integration

Entry A1.1: UAV-Cued Command-Post Authorization and Three-Zone Battle Area
Segmentation

Unlike NATO combined-arms doctrine—which embeds dedicated fire support teams and joint
fires observers inside frontline maneuver companies—Ukrainian ground forces generally do not
deploy embedded fire controllers within combat units. A February 2025 RUSI operational study
observes that, owing to inherited doctrine and organizational culture, neither Ukrainian nor
Russian forces rely on forward-deployed fire controllers; instead, an intelligence and
surveillance enterprise streams real-time UAV video feeds to command posts, where
commanders' representatives validate detections and authorize indirect or drone fires.
To deconflict tube artillery, mortars, strike drones, and long-range fires across unit boundaries,
Ukrainian formations organize the battlespace into three distinct zones. In the Contact Battle
Area, bounded by open terrain along the forward line of own troops (FLOT), battalion
fire-support companies integrate mortars with dedicated FPV drone platoons. These FPV teams
operate in two complementary modes: a static forward line positioned behind infantry fighting
positions engages targets picked up by the battalion's reconnaissance-UAV screen, while
battalion intelligence (G2) and operations (G3) staffs dynamically reposition mobile FPV groups
onto terrain optimized to mass effects against commander-designated priorities. Further back,
Ukrainian forces survey the Middle Battle Area to suppress Russian supporting artillery,
electronic warfare (EW) nodes, and UAV launch teams—isolating frontline Russian infantry from
resupply—while long-range rockets, powered bombs, and missiles target assembly areas and
logistics hubs in the Deep Battle Area.

Entry A1.2: Digital C4ISR Unification (Delta and Kropyva) and Corps-Level
Artillery Brigades (2026)

At the technical liaison layer, Ukrainian fire-support coordination relies on indigenous digital
battle-management software. Janes reports that Ukraine's Delta system is transitioning to
become the sole authorized source for data transfers across the Armed Forces of Ukraine
(AFU), operating alongside Kropyva, a widely adopted battlefield situational awareness
platform supported through Ukraine's Centre of Innovations and Defence Technologies. This
networked architecture enables geographically dispersed guns to mass precision and area
effects remotely across adjacent maneuver boundaries without concentrating firing units
physically.
Organizationally, 2026 force-structure disclosures indicate a sustained transition toward
permanent corps-level organic artillery brigades to improve fire-support unity of command
across multiple maneuver brigades. According to open-source force tracking (MilitaryLand.net)
and official unit portals, the National Guard's 2nd Khartia Corps unveiled its organic 7th Artillery
Brigade ("Hrehit Brigade", Military Unit 3042, commanded by Lieutenant Colonel Vitalii
Korniienko) on April 6, 2026, while the 1st Corps "Azov" stood up the 8th Artillery Brigade
("Harmash") to provide dedicated offensive and defensive fire support. Similarly, Ukrainian
state-affiliated reporting documented the 45th Artillery Brigade operating under the 11th Army
Corps to execute precision HIMARS strikes against Russian Buk air-defense elements.

Rocket and Missile Artillery

Entry A2.1: Western Guided Rocket Artillery (M142 HIMARS, M270/MARS II/LRU)
— Deep Shaping, EW Degradation, and U.S. Dependency

Western multiple-launch rocket systems expanded Ukraine's operational depth beginning in
June 2022, when the United States began delivering truck-mounted M142 HIMARS
launchers—reaching 39 delivered units by January 2024 according to defense-industry
reporting—alongside tracked M270 variants (including German MARS II and French LRU)
supplied by the United Kingdom, Norway, Germany, Italy, and France. Whereas the
C-130-transportable 6×6 HIMARS carries one six-rocket pod (or one 610 mm ATACMS ballistic
missile), the heavier tracked M270 doubles onboard firepower to two pods (12 GMLRS rockets
or two ATACMS) with superior off-road mobility. Firing GPS/INS-guided GMLRS unitary rockets
(capable of hitting within roughly 10 m of an aiming point out to ~80 km, at roughly $160,000 per
rocket), Ukrainian rocket batteries interdicted Russian ammunition dumps, fuel depots, and
Dnipro River crossings in late 2022, directly enabling the Kherson and Kharkiv
counteroffensives. By June 2024, Ukrainian technical teams had adapted broader M270
launchers—beyond France's immediately ATACMS-capable LRU systems—to fire 300 km
ATACMS ballistic missiles against Russian airfields and deep-space communications sites in
Crimea.
Two operational vulnerabilities tempered rocket artillery effectiveness from 2023 onward. First,
pervasive Russian GPS jamming degraded GMLRS terminal accuracy in contested sectors, with
a May 2024 defense-media report citing an assessment that jammed HIMARS rockets
occasionally missed by more than 15 m (50 ft). Nevertheless, characterization of the system as
wholly neutralized is contradicted by continued combat performance: Ukrainian HIMARS units
still destroyed high-value Russian Buk air-defense elements and a tank regiment headquarters
in October 2026. Second, on March 5, 2024, a Russian reconnaissance-drone-cued Iskander
strike scored the first confirmed hit on a Ukrainian HIMARS launcher (variously described in
secondary reporting as destroyed or damaged), demonstrating launcher vulnerability whenever
concealment or shoot-and-scoot discipline lapses. Strategically, CSIS cites Kiel Institute tracking
showing that 86% of Ukraine's rocket artillery systems as of March 2025 were
U.S.-produced, marking HIMARS and its munitions among the hardest capabilities for
European partners to replace as U.S. aid deliveries taper in 2027–2028.

Entry A2.2: Indigenous Guided Rockets and the 2025–2026 Long Neptune Cruise
Missile Campaign

To mitigate partner supply constraints and employment restrictions, Ukraine invested heavily in
indigenous precision rockets and land-attack cruise missiles. Early in the war (first combat use
in May 2022), Ukraine deployed its domestic 300 mm Vilkha-M guided rocket—modified from
Soviet Smerch hardware starting in 2018—which reaches approximately 109 km (68 miles) with
36% greater range and more than twice the warhead mass of GMLRS, though an industry
official told The War Zone in March 2023 that initial production stood at roughly 100 units.
By 2025–2026, Ukraine's long-range strike architecture centered on land-attack variants of the
R-360 Neptune cruise missile operated by the Missile Forces of the Ukrainian Navy. Evolved
from the original 280–300 km, 150 kg-warhead anti-ship missile, the R-360L "Long Neptune"
entered combat service in 2025 with an advertised range up to 1,000 km, a 260 kg warhead,
terrain-following flight, and GNSS-independent navigation, complemented by an
intermediate-range variant informally designated R-360M ("Fat Neptune"). Between August 12
and November 13, 2026, Ukrainian General Staff releases and open-source tracking (compiled
by Defense Express) recorded nine successful Neptune strikes—more than double the
preceding four months—including five within a single 30-day window (October 15–November
13, 2026) hitting Russian Su-34 airbases, solid-propellant and military-electronics factories, and
Rostov-on-Don FPV drone facilities (Ionos LLC and Aero-Hit). Official Ukrainian MoD reporting
on November 3, 2026 confirmed that Ukrainian Navy Neptune missiles destroyed two Su-34
bombers at Marinovka airfield on October 29, while a combined Long Neptune and drone strike
crippled Russian Black Sea Fleet vessels at Novorossiysk on September 9, 2026. Separately,
open-source and media reports indicated that a mixed salvo of Neptune, FP-5 Flamingo, and
Destinus Ruta missiles collapsed a road span exceeding 270 m on the Crimean (Kerch) Bridge
on November 16–17, 2026, followed by a further Neptune strike against the rail section on
November 19. To sustain future missile development, Ukraine's Luch Design Bureau signed a
memorandum of understanding with European missile manufacturer MBDA in June 2026 to
co-develop an enlarged Neptune-2 cruise missile (reported at 6 m length and 50 cm diameter).
Cross-checking strike metadata across sources reveals minor methodological variations in
range and date reporting: Ukraine's Ministry of Defence reported Marinovka airfield at ~300 km
and Kamensky Combine at ~200 km (referencing border offsets) and listed an October strike on
Proton-Electrotex in Oryol (~170 km), whereas Defense Express and OSINT tracking reported
Marinovka at ~430 km and Kamensk-Shakhtinsky at ~190 km from the active front line, and
logged a November 7 Neptune strike on Proton-Electrotex. These variations reflect differences
between straight-line border measurements, front-line launch offsets, and follow-on strikes
rather than conflicting target identities.
Loitering Munitions and Armed Drones

Entry A3.1: Battalion FPV Strike Companies and Heavy Bomber Multicopters —
High Attrition, High Aggregate Share

By 2025–2026, tactical armed UAVs had been institutionalized inside Ukrainian infantry
battalions as an organic fire-support tier complementing mortars and anti-tank guided weapons.
According to RUSI's November 2026 study, a representative Ukrainian battalion strike company
numbers roughly 90 personnel—including approximately 36 FPV pilots backed by 54 infantry,
communications, and munitions specialists. Individual FPV teams consist of four to five soldiers
infiltrating by vehicle with 30–50 FPV drones, batteries, fuzes, munitions, control antennas, and
charging generators for two- to three-day rotations; with 12 teams active concurrently across a
battalion and half firing intensively while the other half rotates, expenditure reaches hundreds of
FPVs per week. Alongside one-way FPVs, Ukrainian forces field heavy reusable bomber
drones adapted from agricultural octocopters (such as the ~$20,000 R18 and the Vampire),
operating primarily at night because of their low speed and hovering release profile to drop
heavier payloads—including explosively formed projectiles, delayed-impact penetrators,
incendiaries, and anti-tank mines.
RUSI field assessments caution against mistaking high aggregate kill shares for high
single-sortie lethality. Between 60% and 80% of Ukrainian FPVs fail to reach their target,
degraded by Russian EW, terrain-induced radio loss beyond 5 km, adverse weather, operator
variance, and radio-frequency interference when multiple drones concentrate simultaneously in
space and time. Among FPVs that do hit, a majority fail to destroy the target—modified armored
vehicles can survive 10 to 15 FPV hits while sustaining repairable damage. Even with these
limitations, massed employment meant tactical UAVs accounted for 60–70% of damaged and
destroyed Russian military systems by early 2025.

Entry A3.2: Middle-Strike One-Way Effectors ("Powered Bombs") and Mass
Procurement Architecture

Distinct from manually piloted FPVs, Ukraine employs autonomous or semi-autonomous
one-way attack drones—designated "powered bombs" by RUSI—that navigate via terrain
elevation tracking, visual odometry, inertial/GNSS guidance, or radio-emission homing to
prosecute point targets in the Middle and Deep Battle Areas. Ukrainian units assess powered
bombs as having a low individual hit rate, high vulnerability to air-defense interception, and
limited destructive payload. Their operational utility depends on strict cost-exchange economics:
when unit cost remains below a low threshold and magazine depth stays high, firing powered
bombs in volume imposes persistent risk across Russian rear areas, forces air-defense radars
to emit, and depletes enemy interceptors—creating penetration corridors for heavier cruise
missiles.
Sustaining this scale has required procurement throughput far beyond peacetime NATO norms.
A November 2025 RUSI supply-chain study notes that combined micro-UAS procurement
across NATO members reached only the tens of thousands through 2025, whereas Ukraine's
modular drone ecosystem—heavily reliant on commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) and
Chinese-origin components alongside 90-day user-developer iteration cycles—operates at
vastly larger volume. By late 2026, Ukraine's Defence Procurement Agency (DOT) was issuing
tenders for long-range attack UAVs under MoD specifications, and a news feed item in
Ukrainian defense-tech outlet Defender Media cited Defence Minister Yevhenii Khmara stating
that Kyiv had allocated €30 billion to purchase 9 million drones in 2026—a single-outlet
trade-media figure that warrants verification against audited budget releases.

Ammunition and Effects

Entry A4.1: Multi-Nature 155 mm Effects, Precision Munitions (Excalibur), and the
EW Countermeasure Cycle

A decisive tactical advantage of Western 155 mm howitzers over UAVs is multi-nature versatility
from a single gun tube: standard high-explosive (HE) shells demolish fortified positions, cluster
rounds suppress dispersed infantry, sensor-fuzed anti-armor rounds (such as BONUS and
SMArt 155) engage moving vehicles, and GPS- or laser-guided shells eliminate point targets.
Early U.S. and Canadian deliveries of M982 Excalibur GPS-guided projectiles reduced shell
expenditure and logistics exposure during the 2022 Donbas and Kherson campaigns.
Yet RUSI's earliest operational assessment warned that modern artillery forces must "fight for
the right to precision" because scarce guided rounds are acutely vulnerable to EW. By May
2024, defense reporting documented that widespread Russian GPS jamming had severely
degraded M982 Excalibur effectiveness, invalidating its peacetime "one shot, one target"
reputation. Effective precision employment therefore depends on deliberate combined-arms
sequencing—using conventional fires, drones, or EW attack to suppress enemy jammers and
open temporary electromagnetic windows before firing GPS-dependent projectiles.

Entry A4.2: Cluster Munitions (DPICM) and the Shift Toward Domestic and
European Shell Output

Having largely exhausted prewar Soviet-caliber artillery stocks by mid-May 2022, Ukraine
became heavily dependent on allied ammunition supplies, with the Kiel Institute estimating that
82% of Ukraine's howitzer ammunition as of March 2025 was U.S.-produced. To offset 155
mm HE shortages and neutralize entrenched Russian infantry during the 2023 counteroffensive,
Ukraine requested and fielded U.S. 155 mm Dual-Purpose Improved Conventional
Munitions (DPICM) (such as M483A1 rounds carrying M77 anti-personnel/anti-armor
submunitions), augmenting Soviet-legacy 220 mm Uragan, 300 mm Smerch, and 203 mm
cluster munitions stockpiled by both sides, as well as Israeli-origin M971 120 mm cluster mortar
rounds photographed in Ukrainian hands.
Simultaneously, Ukraine and European allies accelerated domestic 155 mm industrialization.
According to a 2026 KPMG defense-industry report, Ukraine launched domestic 152 mm shell
production in 2022 and NATO-standard 155 mm production in 2024, supplying roughly half of its
frontline ammunition across calibers domestically by late 2024—reinforced by an October 2024
technology-transfer partnership between Czech defense firm Czechoslovak Group (CSG) and
Ukraine's Ukrainska Bronetechnika for local 155 mm shell manufacturing. Across Europe, the
IISS assessed in September 2026 that European 155 mm production capacity had expanded
roughly fivefold since 2022 and could more than double again by 2030. Major industrial
anchors include Rheinmetall's target of up to 1.5 million 155 mm shells annually by 2027
(driving the European Commission's 2-million-round annual capacity goal), Poland's $662
million domestic 155 mm manufacturing investment (with a secondary defense report citing
Mesko S.A.'s target of 150,000 rounds annually by 2027), and a March 2026 European Defence
Agency initiative reported by commercial market analyst Mordor Intelligence to harmonize 155
mm qualification testing across EU member states.

Target Acquisition

Entry A5.1: Persistent Multicopter Reconnaissance Orbits and EW-Cued
Collection

Target acquisition for Ukrainian indirect fire is sustained by organic aerial observation pushed
down to platoon level. RUSI's November 2026 battalion study records that Ukrainian
reconnaissance teams maintain a standard density of two multicopter ISR relay orbits per
platoon frontage. Across a standard three-company, nine-platoon infantry battalion,
maintaining continuous coverage requires 30 simultaneous orbits (18 covering rifle platoons, 6
managed by company headquarters, and 6 by battalion headquarters), crewed by 36
reconnaissance-UAV operators organic to rifle companies and 24 pilots within a dedicated
battalion reconnaissance platoon. Because commercial-derived multicopters average only
30–35 minutes on station excluding transit time, preserving uninterrupted targeting requires
continuous relay cycling.
Extreme attrition governs this sensor tier: RUSI documented that 90% of employed tactical
UAS are lost, dictating that reconnaissance aircraft remain inexpensive, attritable, and
unit-organic. To sustain orbits under heavy Russian jamming and counter-UAS defenses,
Ukrainian units integrate electronic surveillance and direction-finding (DF) into mission
planning—tracking hostile EW deployments before launching drones and using enemy radio
emissions to cue reconnaissance orbits onto artillery and command targets.

Entry A5.2: Counter-Battery Radars and Passive Acoustic Artillery Detection

Active counter-battery radars remain vital to defeating Russian indirect fire, especially during
poor weather or intense EW when optical UAVs cannot fly. Ukraine operates U.S.-supplied
systems such as the AN/TPQ-36 Firefinder—which electronically scans a 90° horizon sector to
automatically fix hostile artillery and mortar positions—backed by continued U.S. Ukraine
Security Assistance Initiative (USAI) deliveries of counter-artillery and multi-mission radars.
However, active radar emissions expose launchers to rapid Russian counter-fire and loitering
munitions, as evidenced by documented Russian drone strikes destroying Ukrainian
AN/TPQ-36 units.
To reduce electromagnetic exposure, Ukrainian forces increasingly combine active radars with
passive acoustic and electronic direction-finding arrays that detect firing signatures and
enemy UAVs without radiating. Ukraine's domestic defense-tech ecosystem is expanding this
capability; for example, Defender Media reported on November 19, 2026 that Ukrainian firm
Vidar Systems secured $1 million for acoustic artillery detection and is raising further capital to
expand into passive FPV drone detection. (Collection gap: Operational performance metrics and
combat loss logs for European COBRA and ARTHUR counter-battery radars in Ukrainian
service were absent from retrieved open sources and remain a standing collection requirement.)

Technical Fire Direction and Gunnery

Entry A6.1: Remote Automated Fire Control (KRIP-A) and Digital Gun-Level
Laying

Persistent Russian overhead surveillance has driven Ukrainian gunnery toward maximizing crew
standoff and minimizing firing-position dwell times. Donated Western systems introduced
onboard digital ballistics and automated gun-laying—exemplified by Poland's Topaz fire-control
system fielded with AHS Krab battalions and the M777A2 Digital Fire Control System
incorporating the Enhanced Portable Inductive Artillery Fuze Setter (EPIAFS) for programming
Excalibur rounds. Complementing foreign hardware, Ukrainian defense firm Antabos unveiled
the indigenous KRIP-A automated artillery fire-control system in April 2026 (reported by
Ukrainska Pravda), designed specifically to enable remote artillery fire control so gun crews can
lay and fire weapons without remaining exposed at the piece.
(Collection gap: Authoritative technical documentation detailing how Ukrainian Fire Direction
Centers calibrate ballistic tables across heterogeneous 155 mm donor shell and propellant lots
was not present in retrieved open sources—beyond anecdotal forum commentary regarding the
AHS Krab's tolerance for varied ammunition types and industry reporting on the March 2026
EDA 155 mm harmonization effort. Field ballistic-calibration procedures should be verified
against primary Ukrainian or NATO technical manuals before recording quantitative error
margins.)

Entry A6.2: Dispersed Gunnery, Remote Massing of Effects, and Tactical Fire
Deception

Battlefield transparency has rendered classical massed battery positions untenable:
concentrating multiple guns at a single site invites rapid counter-battery destruction. RUSI field
studies demonstrate that Ukrainian technical fire direction preserves survivability through three
core methods—dispersing individual guns widely enough to become uneconomical targets,
displacing rapidly to break Russian kill chains, or operating from deeply dug hardened
hides—while concentrating fire effects remotely across unit boundaries via digital C2.
During offensive operations, however, RUSI warns that overly decentralized target prosecution
undermines operational breakthroughs. A July 2023 RUSI analysis stressed that Ukrainian fire
planners must enforce strict target prioritization to mass sheer weight of fire on decisive breach
axes rather than spending scarce shells on low-priority targets of opportunity, while
incorporating artillery deception—laying deliberate fire plans on secondary sectors to fix and
divert Russian reserves away from the main effort.

Sustainment

Entry A7.1: High Maintenance Tempo, Barrel Wear, and Allied Repair Pipelines

Sustained high-tempo firing inflicts severe mechanical wear on Ukraine's mixed artillery
inventory. RUSI reported in July 2023 that more than one-third of Ukraine's artillery systems
are out of action at any given time for maintenance and repair. Gun tubes function as a
high-consumption expendable: one Ukrainian M777 crew reported firing 6,000 rounds through a
single howitzer over combat service, requiring four barrel replacements. Sustaining readiness
therefore relies on continuous partner deliveries of spare tubes (such as Canada's provision of
10 replacement 155 mm barrels), propellant charges (such as UK packages supplying explosive
charges for more than 90,000 155 mm rounds), recovery vehicles, and dedicated spare-parts
tranches (including £26 million UK spare-parts funding and recurring USAI sustainment
packages). Poland anchors forward heavy-equipment sustainment through regional armored
repair hubs—including facilities established for Leopard tanks and a Poznań hub for Abrams
tanks—alongside expanded domestic 155 mm ammunition capacity. (Collection gap: Primary
documentation detailing Lithuanian depot-level repair hubs for Panzerhaubitze 2000 howitzers
was not present in retrieved open sources and remains an open gap.)

Entry A7.2: Last-Mile Uncrewed Logistics (UGVs/Multicopters) and Post-2026
Sustainment Financing

At the tactical edge, Russian drone and artillery interdiction of ground lines of communication
behind the Contact Battle Area frequently precludes conventional truck resupply. Ukrainian
brigades have adapted by shifting last-mile ammunition and supply delivery onto unmanned
ground vehicles (UGVs) and heavy agricultural multicopters (such as the Vampire), which
carry heavier munitions, FPV batteries, food, water, and medical supplies forward and withdraw
casualties. Illustrating this operational scale, RUSI's November 2026 study recorded a single
sample Ukrainian brigade executing 824 UGV resupply missions in one month (with
individual loads noted as between 100 and 150; exact weight unit truncated in the excerpt).
Because uncrewed logistics platforms suffer high attrition, Ukrainian forces prioritize simplicity,
low cost, and mechanical reliability.
At the strategic level, CSIS projects that U.S. military aid deliveries will begin to dwindle in
early 2027 and sharply diminish in 2028, shifting primary sustainment responsibility to
Europe. While CSIS estimates replacing 2024-level U.S. aid is fiscally manageable for the EU
(an additional 0.12% of EU GDP, or roughly $24 billion nominally), industrial bottlenecks in
rocket artillery and large-caliber shells remain far tighter than fiscal limits. To underwrite
multi-year sustainment, the Council of the EU mobilized sequential financial instruments: two €1
billion European Peace Facility (EPF) ammunition tracks (April 13 and May 5, 2023), the €500
million Act in Support of Ammunition Production (ASAP, July 20, 2023), €3.5 billion in revenues
from immobilized Russian assets channeled via the EPF, a €300 million Ukraine Support
Instrument to expand Ukrainian defense manufacturing, a €90 billion Ukraine Support Loan
finalized on April 23, 2026, and an additional €4.9 billion EPF package on October 26, 2026
(€4.7 billion for member-state aid reimbursement and €230 million for EUMAM Ukraine).
Task B: Training of Gunners, Observers, and Fire
Planners

Entry B1: Multinational External Artillery Pipelines (EUMAM Ukraine,
German Artillery School, U.S. 7th ATC, and Exercise Koalicja 2026)

Because domestic Ukrainian ranges face persistent Russian missile strikes and lack organic
fleets of Western self-propelled and rocket artillery, partner nations have served as Ukraine's
"strategic depth" for specialized artillery qualification since spring 2022. The primary institutional
vehicle is the European Union Military Assistance Mission in support of Ukraine (EUMAM
Ukraine), launched by the Council of the EU in November 2022 with an initial 24-month
mandate, €106.7 million in common costs, 24 contributing member states, and core training
hubs in Germany and Poland. After training more than 100,000 Ukrainian soldiers since 2022
(as summarized from the Council's renewal decision by Danish policy tracker Lovguiden), the
Council of the EU extended EUMAM Ukraine on November 9, 2026 for two further years
through November 15, 2028, allocating nearly €300 million (alongside €230 million in EPF
funding mobilized on October 26, 2026) and establishing a permanent coordination presence
inside Ukraine.
Within EUMAM, specialized heavy-fires training is directed by Germany's multinational Special
Training Command (ST-C) based in Strausberg near Berlin. According to an official February
8, 2023 EEAS report, the German Artillery School in southwestern Germany—the only
institution equipped to qualify Ukrainian crews on the technically complex Panzerhaubitze 2000
self-propelled howitzer, alongside UK- and German-donated MARS II and MLRS rocket
launchers—has trained Ukrainian artillerymen continuously since May 2022. Curricula are
compressed into 10 to 12 training hours per day, focusing on turret operation, automatic
loading systems, primer magazines, and emergency manual reversion drills when autoloaders
malfunction under simulated combat. Upon graduation, crews travel by bus and return directly to
the front line within three days; instructors also incorporate psychological support for
combat-rotated trainees and adapt cross-cultural fire-command communication styles.
Parallel bilateral and coalition pipelines reinforce EUMAM. U.S. forces initiated
platoon-by-platoon HIMARS crew training in summer 2022 (with exact trainee totals withheld for
operational security), and the U.S. Army's 7th Army Training Command conducted
combined-arms training for Ukrainian units at Grafenwöhr, Germany (documented February 17,
2023). Broader foundational infantry and specialist pipelines—including the UK-led Operation
Interflex with 12 partner nations (where Australian instructors served from January 2023 to
mid-2026 before shifting to Operation Legio in Poland in mid-2026) and Czech EUMAM
modules (3,240 personnel trained in 2023; ~4,000 targeted in 2024)—generate maneuver
cadres integrated with fires. Looking toward postwar force regeneration, the French- and UK-led
Multinational Force-Ukraine (MNF-U) under the 37-nation Coalition of the Willing conducted
its first two-week deployment exercise, "Koalicja", beginning November 9, 2026 at Nowa Dęba
in southeastern Poland, deploying approximately 2,800 personnel from 10+ European nations
(including 1,100 French troops, with Polish logistical backing and remote Turkish participation)
to rehearse postwar deployment and Ukrainian military rebuilding.

Entry B2: Domestic Ukrainian Artillery Schools, Compressed
UAV-Observer Courses, and Cadre Trade-Offs

Domestically, officer commissioning and artillery branch education are centered at the Hetman
Petro Sahaidachnyi National Army Academy in Lviv—which conducts officer retraining and
advanced officer courses utilizing the International Center for Peacekeeping and
Security—complemented by the Odesa Military Academy and the 6th Training Artillery
Regiment in Kyiv Oblast.
At the tactical observer tier, UAV-cued gunnery has significantly compressed initial qualification
times relative to traditional forward-observer schooling. RUSI's November 2026 study
documents that reconnaissance-UAV operator training takes approximately two weeks,
enabling infantry battalions to replenish attrition across their 60-operator reconnaissance
establishments. By contrast, FPV strike pilots require substantially greater training investment,
with British Army specialists cited by RUSI noting elevated accident and equipment-failure rates
when instruction is compressed. At the formation-planning level, RUSI's review of Ukraine's
2022–2023 offensive operations highlights a recurring force-generation bottleneck: committing
veteran cadres to hold defensive sectors forced newly raised offensive brigades to rely
predominantly on fresh recruits, leaving an insufficient nucleus of experienced tactical leaders

Analytical Synthesis of Platform Trade-Offs

Combat employment in Ukraine demonstrates why responsiveness outranks raw strategic
lightness on a drone-transparent battlefield. Although the titanium-alloy M777A2 slashed
emplacement and displacement times relative to the legacy M198 (cutting emplacement from 6
min 35 s to 2 min 10 s and displacement from 10 min 40 s to 2 min 23 s), an unarmored towed
detachment needing over two minutes to pack up and hitch to a prime mover remains highly
exposed to Russian counter-battery fire and Lancet/FPV loitering munitions—reflected in at
least 52 M777s reported destroyed or damaged by late May 2023. Conversely, the M120 Rak
delivers organic 120 mm fire support from an armored Rosomak 8×8 turret, halting to fire within
30 seconds and vacating its firing position within 15 seconds of the final round—clearing the
site before enemy counter-battery rounds complete flight. The AHS Krab similarly combines
30-second readiness with an L52 barrel capable of exceeding 50 km using Excalibur precision
rounds, though confirmed Oryx losses of 38 destroyed and 7 damaged vehicles underscore that
armored self-propelled platforms still depend on dispersion, concealment, and counter-UAS
cover to survive.
