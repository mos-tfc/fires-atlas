PLA Artillery and Fire Support:
Reference Supplement, Training,
Lessons Learned, and Source
Verification Audit

This reference supplement fills standing capability, doctrinal, training, and lessons-learned gaps
on People's Liberation Army (PLA) artillery and fire support without repeating baseline entries
on the PHL-191, WS-35/GP155, SLC-2/KVD002, and primary howitzer platforms. Structured
directly around the requested three-task research plan and final verification audit, it delivers 17
modular reference entries—13 across the seven functional gap areas (Task A), 2 covering
institutional schooling and major dated exercises (Task B), and 2 synthesizing competing
assessments of PLA operational lessons learned (Task C)—followed by an opened-source URL
verification registry and baseline errata audit (Final Step).
Three central findings anchor these additions. First, the PLA Ground Force (PLAGF) has built a
layered, digitized fire-support spectrum—from 120 mm battalion gun-mortars and 122 mm
modular rocket pods to 300 mm guided rockets and anti-radiation loitering munitions—that links
close maneuver support directly into theater precision strike. Second, operational-depth rocket
fires rely less on launcher hardware than on vulnerable external enablers: five theater-level
multi-domain intelligence feeds, BeiDou satellite navigation, and rapid mobility to survive
ubiquitous drone surveillance and counter-battery fire. Third, open-source studies published in
2026 reveal a sharp analytical divergence over how effectively PLA artillery adapts to the
Russia-Ukraine war: while Chinese military journals register and pilot discrete tactical
countermeasures (such as fiber-optic drones, capture nets, and jamming guns) within four to
twelve months, institutional inertia, senior-leader purges, and sunk investments in
"intelligentized" AI swarms restrain deeper restructuring toward cheap, attritable mass.
Two methodological caveats govern this supplement. Because the verbatim text of the 12
existing reference entries was not supplied, the errata audit in the Final Step evaluates the most
prevalent factual errors, nomenclature mismatches, and outdated claims circulating in
open-source literature on the referenced baseline systems rather than performing a line-by-line
edit. Additionally, several technical sub-topics—including PLA artillery barrel-wear lifecycles,
manual degraded-mode gunnery drills, brigade-level sound-ranging and meteorological vehicle
specifications, and the post-2024 Information Support Force's tactical fire-support
interfaces—lack verifiable public documentation in accessible open sources and are explicitly
flagged as standing collection gaps.
Research Execution Plan

 1. Step 1 — Task A (Functional Capability Gap-Fill): Compile 1–2 modular reference
 entries across each of seven standing gap areas: Rocket and Missile Artillery (beyond
 PHL-191), Loitering Munitions and Armed Drones, Ammunition and Effects (beyond
 WS-35/GP155), Target Acquisition (beyond SLC-2/KVD002), Technical Fire Direction and
 Gunnery, Counter-Fire and Force Protection, and Sustainment.
 2. Step 2 — Task B (Institutional Training and Exercises): Document PLA artillery
 professional military education (officer and NCO pipelines, campuses, and departments)
 alongside a dated chronology of major artillery and joint firepower exercises from 2009
 through 2025.
 3. Step 3 — Task C (Operational and Conflict Lessons Learned): Synthesize
 open-source reporting on lessons PLA artillery has drawn from internal exercises and the
 Russia-Ukraine war, weighing evidence of rapid technical-tactical absorption against
 structural barriers to institutional adaptation.
 4. Step 4 — Final Step (Source Verification and Baseline Errata Audit): Verify every
 opened source URL, exact page or document title, publisher, and year, record
 source-authority tiers, and identify corrections or currency updates required across
 commonly cited baseline PLA artillery entries.
Task A: Capability and Functional Gap-Fill Entries

Area 1: Rocket and Missile Artillery (Beyond PHL-191)

PLA rocket artillery functions as an echeloned reach architecture spanning 13 km to 500 km
rather than an isolated fires branch. Mapping active platforms by organizational tier
demonstrates how the systems documented below fill the critical 40–160 km operational band
separating combined arms brigade cannon artillery from the group army's cross-strait
PCH-191/PHL-16 tier.

Entry A1: PHL-03 (300 mm) Long-Range Multiple Rocket Launcher

Fielded in 2006 as a Chinese derivative of the Russian BM-30 Smerch, the PHL-03 serves as
the numerical mainstay of PLA group army long-range rocket fires beneath the newer PCH-191,
with open-source equipment tallies estimating roughly 175 launchers in service. Each 12-barrel
truck-mounted launcher discharges a full salvo in under one minute to deliver heavy area
suppression or guided precision strikes against operational-depth force concentrations and
coastal infrastructure. Whereas legacy 300 mm munitions spanned 70–150 km depending on
rocket type, upgraded guided rockets have extended reach from 130 km to 160 km, with
export-grade guided rounds claiming a Circular Error Probable (CEP) of approximately 30
meters.
Organizationally, the PHL-03 shifted from concentrated specialists to ubiquitous corps-level
distribution during the 2017 PLA restructuring. After legacy artillery divisions in the former 1st
and 42nd Group Armies were converted into standalone Long-Range Rocket Brigades
(LRRBs), the 2017 reforms dissolved those dedicated LRRBs and redistributed PHL-03
battalions into newly organized group army artillery brigades, ensuring that every group army
artillery brigade now holds a 300 mm PHL-03 battalion alongside specialized coastal
deployments such as the Eastern Theater Command's 303rd Coastal Defense Brigade on
Pingtan Island. Operationally, however, the PHL-03 faces two persistent constraints: an
approximately one-hour reload cycle that slows salvo tempo and exposes launchers during
resupply, and a ~150–160 km reach ceiling that restricts Pingtan-based units to a limited stretch
of northern Taiwan's western shore—directly spurring development of the 350–500 km
PCH-191. Even so, the PLAA has expanded PHL-03 targeting versatility via UAV cueing: the
80th Group Army conducted UAV-supported multi-wave strikes in June 2021, and the 79th
Group Army demonstrated PHL-03 strikes against moving maritime targets in early 2021.

Entry A2: Brigade-Level 122 mm Modular Rocket Artillery (PHL-11, PHZ-11,
PHL-20, PHL-21) and Tactical VLS Launchers (CM-501GA)

Within Combined Arms Brigade (CABde) artillery battalions, the PLAGF has progressively
replaced legacy manually loaded 122 mm launchers (PHL-81 and PHZ-89) with modular,
pod-loaded systems tailored to wheeled, tracked, and light maneuver formations. Derived from
NORINCO's export SR-4, the wheeled PHL-11 (a 20-ton, 6×6 Shaanxi SX2190KA truck with a
3-person crew and 80 km/h road speed, according to secondary technical profiles) and the
tracked PHZ-11 (sharing a tracked chassis related to the PLZ-05 and PGZ-09) each mount two
20-tube containerized rocket pods totaling 40 barrels. Open-source technical specifications
credit the PHL-11 with firing a 40-rocket salvo in 30 seconds to an effective range of 40 km and
a maximum extended range of 50 km, supported by an onboard fire-control computer and crew
CBRN protection. Crucially, pod-swapping cut reload time in half from 10 minutes to 5 minutes.
According to IISS Military Balance 2019 data cited in secondary literature, PLAGF inventories
included 350 PHL-11s, 100 PHZ-11s, and 375 legacy PHZ-89s; subsequent open-source tallies
as of 2022 estimate roughly 350 PHL-11s and 120 PHZ-11s in service.
For light, plateau, and rapid-reaction brigades, NORINCO introduced scaled-down single-pod
launchers derived from the export SR-7: the PHL-20 (mounted on a 6×6 assault chassis
believed to be the Dongfeng Mengshi; ~30 units estimated as of 2022) and the PHL-21
(mounted on a 4×4 FAW MV3/CTM-133 truck sharing chassis commonality with the PCL-161
howitzer; 10+ units estimated as of 2022), alongside a comparable 4×4 20-tube modular
launcher observed in the Tibet Military District. Depending on pod configuration, SR-7-derived
launchers fire either twenty 122 mm rockets (up to 50 km) or six 220 mm rockets reaching 70
km, significantly expanding organic brigade deep-fire reach. Beyond conventional rockets,
Taiwan's Institute for National Defense and Security Research (INDSR) notes PLA ground
integration of 6×6 vehicles fitted with Vertical Launch Systems (VLS) for the CM-501GA tactical
cruise missile, further narrowing the divide between brigade artillery and precision missile strike.

Area 2: Loitering Munitions and Armed Drones

Entry A3: Anti-Radiation Loitering Munitions — ASN-301 and Harpy Heritage

China's anti-radiation loitering munition lineage traces directly to its purchase of approximately
100 Israeli IAI Harpy autonomous Suppression of Enemy Air Defenses (SEAD) drones between
1994 and 1999. After U.S. diplomatic pressure prompted Israel to cancel a 2004 upgrade
agreement, the Xi'an ASN Technology Group developed an indigenous near-copy designated
the ASN-301, publicly unveiled in 2017—including an August 2017 military parade appearance
in a 54-unit formation cited by a December 2025 Centre for Joint Warfare Studies (CENJOWS)
monograph. According to that monograph, the ASN-301 incorporates a passive radar-homing
seeker with a 25 km search range and a 7,000-fragment warhead triggered by a proximity
laser fuze with a 20-meter destructive radius.
Doctrinally, the ASN-301 provides an autonomous counter-radar fires layer that complements
rocket and missile barrages by loitering over contested battlespace to destroy or force
shutdowns of adversary air-defense and weapon-locating radars. Although CENJOWS
assesses that ASN-301 formations are most likely subordinated to PLAAF Electronic
Countermeasures (ECM) regiments, official Eastern Theater Command footage reported by
defense media in 2025 showed a live-fire test of the ASN-301, confirming active operational
integration into theater-level joint strike planning facing Taiwan and regional allied defenses.

Entry A4: Tactical Loitering Munitions, Swarm Launchers, and Armed
Recon-Strike UAS (CH-901/FH-901, CM-501XA, CH-4)
Beneath dedicated anti-radiation drones, China's defense sector has fielded tactical loitering
munitions designed to deliver organic precision fires where conventional tube artillery cannot
reach. Prominent among these is the China Aerospace Science and Technology Corporation
(CASC) CH-901 / FH-901 family—a compact, twin-wing, vertical-tail munition with a
nose-mounted electro-optical navigation and targeting pod. Demonstrations by the China
Academy of Electronics and Information Technology displayed CH-901-style loitering munitions
salvo-launched from vehicle-mounted arrays of tubular launchers for swarm employment.
Open-source defense analyses indicate that select PLA formations have procured the
CH-901/FH-901—most likely Special Operations Forces (SOF), amphibious assault units,
and airborne troops that lack assured conventional air or heavy artillery support during initial
lodgments. Additional domestic tactical loitering munitions include the 6×6 VLS-launched
CM-501XA, NORINCO's Cruise Dragon / Loitering Dragon 10A, WS-43, and BG-201, though
unit-level PLAGF inventories remain undisclosed in public sources.
In the medium-altitude armed reconnaissance-strike tier—beyond the PLAA's KVD002, which
official CCTV footage reported by Global Times in July 2026 showed conducting live-fire strikes
against a moving maritime vessel off southeast China—the PLA Army operates the CH-4
unmanned aerial system within the Eastern Theater Command. According to the U.S. Naval
War College's China Maritime Studies Institute (CMSI), the CH-4 serves as a vital army sensor
to acquire deep targets in real time, advise commanders on munition selection, direct PCH-191
precision rocket strikes, and perform post-strike Battle Damage Assessment (BDA). However,
CMSI warns that PLAA platforms such as the CH-4 remain vulnerable to integrated air-defense
networks like Taiwan's, mirroring broader survivability limits observed among non-stealthy
Chinese MALE UCAVs in contested foreign airspace. In parallel, China continues developing
dedicated swarm-carrier architectures, highlighted by Jane's reporting on the maiden flight of
the Jiutian swarm-launching mothership UAV.

Area 3: Ammunition and Effects (Beyond WS-35 and GP155)

Entry A5: Cluster Munitions (DPICM/Submunitions) and PRC Non-Signatory
Policy

Unlike states party to the 2008 Convention on Cluster Munitions (CCM), the People's Republic
of China is not a signatory to either the CCM or the Mine Ban Treaty, citing "national defence
needs" as its rationale for remaining outside the ban. While Beijing attends CCM review
conferences as an observer, voted in favor of UN General Assembly Resolution 76/47
promoting the convention in December 2021 (after abstaining annually from 2015 to 2020), and
asserts it has never used cluster munitions in combat, China acknowledges to the Landmine
and Cluster Munition Monitor that it produces, stockpiles, and exports an extensive array of
artillery, rocket, air-dropped, and ballistic-missile cluster munitions.
Domestic production spans China's primary defense conglomerates. NORINCO manufactures
conventional cannon and rocket Dual-Purpose Improved Conventional Munition (DPICM)
carriers across every legacy and modern PLA caliber (107 mm through 203 mm), while China
Aerospace Science and Industry Corporation (CASIC) and its subsidiary Sichuan Aerospace
Industry Corporation produce heavy unguided and tactical rocket carriers—including the 302
mm WS-1/-1B/-1E, 320 mm WS-2, 300 mm SY300, and 400 mm SY400—offering DPICM,
Combined Effects Munitions (CEM), and Sensor-Fuzed Weapons (SFW). Submunition
warheads are also documented for multiple Chinese ballistic missiles, including the DF-11,
DF-15, DF-16B (800–1,000 km range), DF-21, and M-7. Although China publishes no export
transparency data, demining clearance has confirmed foreign combat use of Chinese artillery
submunitions: unexploded Type-81 DPICM bomblets were cleared in Iraq in 2003, and
Hezbollah fired more than 100 Chinese Type-81 122 mm rockets carrying Type-90 (MZD-2)
DPICM submunitions into northern Israel in July–August 2006. Retaining unrestricted DPICM
and sensor-fuzed stockpiles gives PLA fire planners an area-saturation and anti-armor multiplier
against dispersed maneuver and air-defense targets where unitary high-explosive rounds
require far higher shell expenditure.

Entry A6: Extended-Range Conventional, Airburst, 122 mm Guided, and
Maritime-Strike Munitions

Beyond the 155 mm WS-35 and GP155/GP1 baseline, PLA indirect-fire effects span four
complementary munition tiers that broaden target-set coverage without exhausting strategic
ballistic missile inventories:
 ● Extended-Range Unitary 155 mm and 122 mm Projectiles: Open-source technical
 profiles report that 52-caliber 155 mm howitzers (PLZ-05 and PCL-181) fire Extended
 Range Full Bore–Base Bleed (ERFB-BB) shells to 39 km and Rocket-Assisted Projectiles
 (ERFB-BB-RA / VLAP-class) to 50–53 km. At the combined arms brigade 122 mm tier,
 secondary technical references report that the wheeled PLL-09 reaches 18 km with
 standard HE-FRAG projectiles and 27 km with extended-range shells.
 ● Expanded Precision Cannon Family (GP2, GP6/GP155A, GP155B): NORINCO trade
 documentation confirms that China's laser-homing lineage extends down to the 122 mm
 caliber via the GP2 laser-guided projectile. At 155 mm, secondary defense reporting
 tracks an expanded family beyond the baseline 4–20 km GP1/GP155 (52 kg, 1,305 mm,
 per NORINCO trade literature cited by Armament Research Services) to include the
 improved laser-homing GP6 (GP155A) and the satellite-guided GP155B.
 ● Long-Range Airburst and Heavy Pre-Fragmented Rocket Warheads: Jane's reported
 Chinese military media footage showing the PLAGF's PHL-16 / PCH-191 firing airburst
 munitions—an effect well suited to suppressing exposed personnel, radar arrays, and
 soft-skinned logistics sites. At the heavy tactical missile tier, INDSR reports that the 750
 mm Fire Dragon 480 (7.38 meters long) carries a 480 kg
 high-explosive/pre-fragmented warhead configurable for multiple specialized warhead
 payloads out to a claimed 500 km.
 ● Organic PLAA Anti-Ship Munitions: Integrating the TL-7B anti-ship cruise missile
 onto the PCH-191 launcher provides the PLA Army with its first organic anti-ship fire
 effect, enabling ground artillery brigades to contest maritime approaches in the Taiwan
 Strait alongside PLAN and PLARF units.

Area 4: Target Acquisition (Beyond SLC-2 and KVD002)

Entry A7: Group Army Target Support Battalions and Complementary
Ground/Tactical Sensors

Following the 2017 restructuring, PLA group army artillery brigades established a dedicated
target support element responsible for deep target acquisition, matching munitions to target
characteristics, and performing post-strike BDA. While the SLC-2 phased-array weapon-locating
radar—an improved derivative of the U.S. TPQ-37 credited in open-source defense discussions
with detecting incoming artillery out to ~50 km—anchors counter-battery acquisition, secondary
defense blogs note that it operates alongside newer Type 704-series counter-battery radars.
Translated PLA joint information-operations lectures further reference specialized
meteorological, gun-directing, and fire-control radars alongside acoustic confrontation
methods. For tactical aerial observation, CASI (2018) notes that PLA artillery historically relied
on ASN-series UAVs (~150 km range) streaming video imagery to adjust fire and evaluate
BDA, supported by dedicated decoy (RKL-165) and communications-relay (TK-J226) variants.
Standing Open-Source Gap: Although PLA doctrinal translations acknowledge acoustic
confrontation and meteorological/survey radars, and U.S. Army ATP 7-100.3: Chinese Tactics
catalogs doctrinal artillery brigade and battalion fire-support capabilities in Chapter 2 and
Appendix B, verifiable open-source technical specifications for current post-2017 PLA
sound-ranging arrays, electro-optical reconnaissance vehicles, and mobile ballistic
meteorological stations were unavailable in the opened documentation.

Entry A8: Theater-Level Multi-Domain Intelligence Integration for Deep Fires

For long-range rocket battalions (PHL-03 and PCH-191) engaging targets deep inside
adversary territory, organic ground artillery reconnaissance teams cannot physically deploy
forward to observe impacts, and organic army reconnaissance UAS (such as the CH-4) face
severe attrition within integrated air-defense networks. To close this operational sensor gap,
CMSI documents that PLA deep-fire rocket units integrate into a theater-level joint
intelligence network fusing five external intelligence feeds under Theater Joint Operations
Command Center (JOCC) control.
Under this theater command architecture, PCH-191 long-range rocket battalions can
theoretically operate under the JOCC's Land Operations Sub-Center (LOSC) to support
coastal landing and maneuver forces, or be task-assigned directly to the Conventional Missile
Operations Sub-Center (CMOSC) alongside PLARF brigades for theater-wide joint firepower
strikes. This flexible subordination elevates army artillery into a scalable strategic-operational
strike arm, though its effectiveness remains tightly coupled to the survivability of theater C4ISR
links against adversary cyber and electronic warfare.

Area 5: Technical Fire Direction and Gunnery

Entry A9: Digitized On-Gun Ballistic Computing, Automatic Laying, MRSI, and ICP
Integration

Modern PLA artillery gunnery has transitioned from manual, labor-intensive battery fire direction
toward distributed, platform-level digital fire control networked into higher-echelon C2 systems.
Both the tracked PLZ-05/PLZ-05A (export PLZ-52) and the wheeled PCL-181 (export SH-15)
incorporate onboard digital fire-control computers, automatic gun-laying systems, and automatic
or semi-automatic loaders, while open-source imagery analyses note a barrel-mounted muzzle
velocity radar on the PLZ-05. On the PCL-181, once an operator enters target azimuth and
coordinates, the vehicle computer calculates the ballistic trajectory and automatically lays the
52-caliber gun while hydropneumatic suspension stabilizes the truck chassis and expands
elevation/depression angles.
This platform-level automation compresses engagement timelines and enables high-tempo fire
effects. Secondary technical profiles credit the PLZ-05 with a burst rate of 4 rounds in 15
seconds (8–10 rounds/min maximum) and note that the export PLZ-52 supports Multiple
Round Simultaneous Impact (MRSI)—firing up to 4 rounds along sequentially adjusted
elevations to impact the same target simultaneously. At the precision-strike layer, PCL-181,
PHL-03, and PCH-191 guided munitions pair inertial navigation with the BeiDou Navigation
Satellite System (BDS), achieving claimed CEPs near 30 meters on long-range rockets. Above
the individual firing piece, PLA units link into the Integrated Command Platform (ICP)—an
automated C4ISR computer backbone fielded down to tactical echelons to unify C2, sensor
feeds, and fire-control orders across branches and services.
Standing Open-Source Gap: While open English-language sources verify digital fire-control
computers and meteorological radars, primary open-source documentation is lacking on PLA
battery-level Fire Direction Center (FDC) computational workflows, standardized meteorological
message formats, and manual degraded-mode gunnery procedures under sustained BeiDou
and ICP jamming.

Area 6: Counter-Fire and Force Protection

Entry A10: Wheeled Digitization, Active Transit Defense ("Four Antis"), and
Coastal Counter-Fire

PLA artillery survivability prioritizes compressing emplacement and displacement times to defeat
adversary counter-battery radars and precision counter-fire. Secondary technical profiles note
that legacy towed howitzers—such as the 152 mm PL-66 and 130 mm Type 59-1—required
roughly 3 minutes to emplace and 2–3 minutes to displace with crews of 6 to 10 soldiers.
Replacing legacy towed pieces with the 25-ton, 90 km/h PCL-181 (air-transportable via Y-9), the
Mengshi-mounted PCL-171, and modular PHL-11/PCH-191 rocket launchers enables rapid
shoot-and-scoot cycles and wide geographic dispersal. Open-source threat assessments
emphasize that heavy launchers like the PCH-191 generate pronounced launch signatures,
making constant displacement and dispersion essential to survive counter-fire and air attack.
During long-range operational movement, major PLA exercises (documented from Mission
Action-2013 onward) enforce four concurrent active defense drills—the "Four Antis":
anti-reconnaissance, anti-air raid, anti-jamming, and anti-harassment. Doctrinally, U.S.
Army ATP 7-100.3 and TRADOC G-2 (2025) note that PLA artillery groups dynamically shift
between direct maneuver support and dedicated counter-fire, obstacle reduction, and
disruption; in island and littoral defense operations, PLA Army coastal defense artillery provides
sustained counter-fire to disrupt adversary fire support and deny landing forces time to build
combat power.

Entry A11: Integrated Organic Air Defense and Emerging Counter-UAS (C-UAS)
Layers

To protect firing units and support hubs from aerial attack, each PLA group army fields an
organic air-defense brigade and each combined arms brigade fields an organic air-defense
battalion armed with tracked anti-aircraft guns and short-range surface-to-air missiles. Chinese
military media cited in CASI's May 2026 PLA Primer specifically highlight HQ-17A mobile
short-range SAM systems operating in direct coordination with PHL-191 long-range rocket units
to provide an accompanying air-defense shield.
More recently, the ubiquity of tactical FPV and reconnaissance drones in Ukraine has
accelerated ground-level C-UAS adaptation. According to CSIS (October 2026), 2024 PLA unit
training footage—explicitly framed by Chinese outlets as drawing on Russia-Ukraine combat
experience—featured ground forces employing capture nets, handheld jamming guns, and
rapid evasion drills. At the equipment level, China is exploring dedicated layered C-UAS
platforms, including a laser-equipped Type 19 IFV variant reported by Jane's (though Jane's
cautions it remains unclear whether the vehicle is operational, in trials, or a technology
demonstrator) and mobile 76 mm counter-drone gun concepts (LD76) aimed at defending
maneuver units as well as vulnerable ammunition and maintenance hubs.

Area 7: Sustainment

Entry A12: Organic Battery Resupply Vehicles, Chassis Commonality, and Reload
Bottlenecks

High-tempo digitized artillery depends on resupply vehicles able to keep pace with
self-propelled firing units in contested terrain. At the heavy tracked tier, the PLZ-05 family
(including the lengthened seven-roadwheel PLZ-05B) pairs with a dedicated armored tracked
ammunition resupply vehicle designed to transfer rounds under armor on the battlefield. In
light combined arms brigades, open-source unit breakdowns describe a PCL-171 122 mm
battery operating at a 1:1 firing-to-resupply ratio—9 PCL-171 firing vehicles (each carrying up
to 28 rounds) supported by 9 CTL-181A ammunition resupply vehicles—capitalizing on the
shared 6×6 Dongfeng Mengshi CTL-181A chassis (3.65-ton payload) used across light and
medium brigade air-defense, mortar, and bridging variants to streamline field maintenance. For
onboard ready-rack endurance, secondary technical sources credit the PCL-181 with 30 rounds
and the PLL-05 120 mm gun-mortar with 36 rounds.
Reloading mechanics create a marked operational divergence across rocket echelons.
Containerized pod reloads on the 122 mm PHL-11 reduced reload duration from 10 minutes to 5
minutes. By contrast, tube-by-tube reloading on the 300 mm PHL-03 still takes roughly one
hour, imposing a significant tempo constraint and counter-battery vulnerability window during
sustained fire missions.

Entry A13: Joint Logistics Architecture (PLAJLSF), Civil-Military Transport, and
Magazine Depth

Above the group army's organic service support and sustainment brigade, operational and
theater-level ammunition supply is coordinated through the PLA Joint Logistics Support
Force (PLAJLSF), established during the post-2015 CMC reforms (created in September, per
Japan's National Institute for Defense Studies) to unify joint theater sustainment. Trans-regional
exercises (Stride-2009, Mission Action-2013) have repeatedly tested civil-military integration
(CMI) in force sustainment—integrating civilian rail, air, maritime, and motorized transport assets
alongside local bulk material procurement to sustain brigades deployed an average of 1,200 km
from home garrisons.
Even with joint logistics reforms, U.S. Army TRADOC G-2 (2025) identifies sustainment as a
major operational friction point for PLA fires: the physical size and heavy logistical footprint of
traditional tube and rocket artillery complicate expeditionary deployment and sustainment, while
the bulk and support demands of ballistic missile units keep them anchored to mainland launch
complexes. Although TRADOC assesses overall PLA missile and munition magazine depth as
substantial—and notes that shore-based short-range cruise missiles could help offset potential
ballistic-missile shortfalls—it concludes that open sources provide insufficient data to
determine exact wartime magazine depth or later-phase sustainment capacity. Open-source
reporting likewise lacks verifiable metrics on PLA artillery barrel-wear lifecycles and field
tube-replacement pipelines.
Task B: Artillery Education, Training, and Exercises

Entry B1: PLA Army Academy of Artillery and Air Defense (陆军炮兵防
空兵学院)

The central institution educating PLA artillery officers, fire planners, and noncommissioned
officers is the PLA Army Academy of Artillery and Air Defense (中国人民解放军陆军炮兵防
空兵学院), established in June 2017 under the CMC's military-education consolidation (which
reduced total PLA academic institutions from 63 officer and 4 NCO schools to 34 officer and 3
NCO institutions, while ending recruitment for the civilian National Defense Student Program in
2017 for full phase-out by mid-2020). The academy merged four legacy schools into a single
PLAGF-subordinate four-campus organization:
 ● Hefei Campus (Main Campus, Anhui): Formerly the Army Command College
 (historically the Artillery Command College), serving as academy headquarters.
 ● Nanjing Campus (Jiangsu): Formerly the Nanjing Artillery Academy, dedicated to field
 artillery command and technical gunnery.
 ● Zhengzhou Campus (Henan): Formerly the PLA Air Defense Corps Academy, training
 ground air-defense junior command officers and cadets.
 ● Shenyang Campus (Liaoning): Houses the academy's subordinate NCO School,
 training enlisted gunners, observers, and fire-control technicians.
Open-source institutional profiles list a faculty of 1 academician, 4 specialist teaching major
generals, 184 professors, and 305 associate professors delivering undergraduate, graduate,
officer command, and NCO instruction across specialized departments (xi) including the
Artillery Subunit Command Department (炮兵分队指挥系), Antitank Missile Subunit
Command Department, Air Defense Missile Technology and Command Department, and
degree programs such as Firepower Command & Control Engineering (火力指挥与控制工
程). Curriculum execution incorporates live-fire graduation exercises (such as a Zhengzhou
Campus graduating cadet live-fire drill reported in June 2021) as well as direct involvement in
testing operational long-range rocket systems (reported in May 2021).
Despite institutional consolidation, independent PLA analysts (including Dennis Blasko's
U.S.-China Economic and Security Review Commission testimony) identify persistent
cross-branch training gaps. Because post-2017 combined arms battalions integrate companies
and platoons from up to 10 distinct branches (armor, infantry, artillery, air defense, and others),
many battalion and company commanders have not received formal training in the employment
of newly subordinated branch capabilities, and advanced strike systems have periodically
arrived at units ahead of finished manuals and qualified instructors.

Entry B2: Major PLA Artillery and Joint Firepower Exercises
(2009–2025)
Over the past 17 years, PLA artillery exercises shifted from scripted home-garrison live-fire

Task C: Operational and Conflict Lessons Learned

Open-source assessments published in 2026 diverge sharply on how effectively PLA artillery
and fire-support communities translate foreign combat observations into force-wide adaptation.
Capturing both poles of this debate—fast technical-tactical observation versus deep institutional
friction—prevents either underestimating or overestimating PLA learning agility.

Entry C1: Rapid Tactical-Technical Absorption from Ukraine ("Living
Textbook") and Exercise After-Action Reviews

An October 2026 Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) study by Lilly Min-Chen
Lee and Kateryna Bondar—examining more than 90 verified Chinese-language sources across
45 outlets (including PLA Daily and journals of Chinese missile, shipbuilding, and aviation
conglomerates) from July 2022 to August 2026—finds that Chinese military analysts treat the
Russia-Ukraine war as a "living textbook" and call 2024 "year one of world drone war".
According to CSIS, the PLA's analytical intake is unusually rapid: newly fielded frontline
techniques appear in Chinese professional publications within an average of four months,
while translation into unit training and hardware prototypes generally takes one year or more.
Four artillery-relevant lessons recur across PLA commentary and parallel Western assessments
(including Royal United Services Institute [RUSI] analyses):
 1. Compressed Reconnaissance-Strike Kill Chains: Analysts at the PLA National
 Defense University's Joint Operations College note Russian forces striking detected
 targets within 3 to 5 minutes (versus hours in earlier wars), while PLA unit researchers
 credit Russia's reconnaissance-strike complex—pushing low-cost Orlan-10
 reconnaissance drones deep into enemy rear areas to cue artillery and Kub or Lancet
 loitering munitions. The PLA's primary emulation goal is directly wiring drones into artillery
 and command networks.
 2. Economic Mass of Drone-Enabled Fires: A March 2025 PLA Daily article treated as
 settled fact that a single reconnaissance-strike drone costs less than 1 percent of a
 modern fighter while delivering more than 90 percent of its battlefield coverage,
 framing the >1 million small drones procured by Russia and Ukraine in 2024 as the
 structural baseline of modern ground combat rather than ad hoc improvisation.
 3. Electronic Warfare, Fiber-Optic Workarounds, and Ground C-UAS: Recognizing
 severe drone attrition to EW jamming (estimated by RUSI at roughly 90% overall UAS
 loss rates in Ukraine), PLA Daily highlighted Russia's jamming-immune fiber-optic FPV
 drones in May 2024—weeks after combat debut—and by April 2025 CCTV broadcast a
 People's Armed Police unit in Yunnan training with FPV drones and researching
 fiber-optic models. Concurrently, 2024 PLA ground training footage incorporated
 Ukraine-derived capture nets, jamming guns, and anti-drone evasion drills.
 4. Dispersion, Logistics Depth, and Internal Command Friction: Ukrainian combat
 lessons—where Russia struck 75% of static Ukrainian defense sites within 48 hours and
 Ukrainian HIMARS/ATACMS strikes forced Russian forces to disperse logistics more than
 50 km behind the front line at a permanent efficiency penalty—reinforce PLA investments
 in wheeled self-propelled artillery and dispersed logistics. Internally, however, PLA
 exercise after-action reviews continue to cite persistent command weaknesses
 summarized as the "Five Incapables" (inability to judge situations, understand higher
 intent, make operational decisions, deploy troops, and handle unexpected contingencies)
 alongside weak cross-unit information sharing.

Entry C2: Institutional Inertia and Selective Learning
("Lessons-Learned with Chinese Characteristics")

Counterbalancing CSIS's portrait of a fast learning pipeline, an April 2026 Institute for the Study
of War (ISW) analysis published via the American Enterprise Institute (AEI) by Amy Sinnenberg
and Jackson Karas argues that PLA adaptation is heavily distorted by preconception bias,
sunk technological investments, and political risk aversion.
First, because Xi Jinping elevated "intelligentization" (智能化)—integrating AI into kill chains,
C2, and logistics—as a premier modernization mandate in 2020, the PLA invested heavily prior
to 2022 in large, exquisite, survivable drones (Wing Loong-2, GJ-11, CH-4) and AI-driven
swarming technology. Although actual combat in Ukraine and the Middle East has relied
overwhelmingly on masses of low-cost, attritable FPVs and one-way attack drones (such as
Shaheds) rather than autonomous AI swarms, PLA Daily articles continue to treat cheap
attritable systems as non-characteristic counterexamples and insist the next stage of warfare
centers on sophisticated AI swarms.
Second, while the PLA recognizes the severe operational toll of EW and
satellite-communications denial in Ukraine (noting Russian struggles after losing Starlink
access), Chinese writers largely expect future AI autonomy to overcome EW disruption rather
than restructuring units around high-attrition, expendable drone inventories. Third, even when
evaluating Russian institutional models such as the "Rubikon" Center for Advanced
Unmanned Technologies (built to cycle frontline feedback directly to developers), PLA
analyses selectively omit Rubikon's tactical deployment of massed, low-cost attritable strike
systems. Finally, Xi's ongoing purge campaign (removing over 100 senior officials since 2022
and shrinking the Central Military Commission) has fostered an institutional culture prioritizing
political loyalty over bottom-up tactical dissent, making deviation from top-down modernization
narratives professionally and personally hazardous. Taken together, the CSIS and ISW/AEI
studies indicate a bifurcated PLA artillery force: agile at copying discrete counter-drone gear
and sensor-to-shooter links, yet structurally slow to discard centralized, high-end
doctrinal assumptions.
