India: Artillery and Fire Support Reference

Research date: 24 November 2026

PART A: OVERVIEW

 1. Army name: Indian Army (Regiment of Artillery). The Indian Air Force and Indian Navy
 operate air-launched and naval strike weapons, including BrahMos, but land-based tube,
 rocket, and surface-to-surface fire support belongs to the Indian Army.
 2. Theme: Massed 155mm Mediumisation, Indigenous Rockets and Mountain Firepower
 3. Description: The Indian Army organises its fire support under the Regiment of Artillery,
 one of its largest combat arms, structured into regiments supporting infantry, armoured,
 and mountain formations. Since the 1999 Kargil conflict, doctrine has prioritised
 "mediumisation"—standardising on 155mm calibre guns—and indigenisation under the
 Field Artillery Rationalisation Plan (FARP). Its fire support is distinct for balancing high-
 altitude mountain warfare requirements against desert and plains mechanised
 operations across two active borders. Recent reforms integrate indigenous rockets,
 digital fire control, and loitering munitions into dedicated artillery formations.
 4. Order of battle (as of September–November 2026):
 ○ Army / Corps level: Artillery Divisions and Independent Artillery Brigades
 operating long-range rocket and missile systems (Pinaka, BM-30 Smerch,
 Suryastra PULS) and counter-fire radars (Swathi WLR).
 ○ Division level: Divisional Artillery Brigades (typically 3–5 regiments) fielding
 155mm towed (FH-77B Bofors, Dhanush, Sharang, M777, ATAGS on order),
 155mm self-propelled (K9 Vajra-T), and legacy 105mm/122mm/130mm field and
 medium regiments.
 ○ Regiment / Battery level: Artillery Regiments (typically 18 guns across 3
 batteries of 6 guns, or 18 rocket launchers per regiment), plus emerging
 Shaktibaan regiments and Divyastra batteries integrating loitering munitions.
 ○ Active inventory (Reported as of 23 September 2026): 4,856 active artillery
 systems—4,436 towed pieces (including 2,400 Indian Field Guns 105mm, 660
 M-46 130mm, 520 D-30 122mm, 300 FH-77B, 180 M-46 Soltam, 159 Sharang,
 145 M777, 72 Dhanush), 100 K9 Vajra-T self-propelled howitzers, and 320 rocket
 launchers (150 Agnibaan/Grad, 126 Pinaka, 42 Smerch, 2 Suryastra PULS).
 5. Region: South Asia (new region).

Entry 1
 1. Title: Regiment of Artillery Organisation
 2. Area: Liaison and Fire Support Integration
 3. Section: Forces
 4. Force: Indian Army
 5. Echelon: Regiment / Brigade, Division, Corps / Army
 6. What: The Regiment of Artillery is the Indian Army's single fire-support arm, providing
 tube, rocket, surveillance, and target acquisition assets across all operational formations.
 7. How:
 1. Regiments are grouped into Artillery Brigades at division level and Artillery
 Divisions at corps or army level.
 2. Each field or medium regiment typically fields three firing batteries.
 3. Rocket regiments operate multi-barrel launchers such as Pinaka and Smerch for
 deep area fires.
 4. Surveillance and Target Acquisition (SATA) units provide radar, drone, and
 acoustic locating support.
 8. Why: Centralising fire support under one arm allows the Indian Army to mass fires
 rapidly at decisive points, as demonstrated during the 1999 Kargil conflict.
 9. Compare with the US: Like the US Army Field Artillery Branch, it centralises doctrine
 and training, though Indian artillery retains a heavier proportion of towed regiments.

Entry 3

 1. Title: Field Artillery Rationalisation Plan (FARP)
 2. Area: Guns, Howitzers and Mortars
 3. Section: Equipment
 4. Force: Indian Army
 5. Echelon: Corps / Army
 6. What: Approved in 1999, FARP is India's long-term programme to standardise its
 artillery on 155mm calibre systems across towed, mounted, ultra-light, and tracked
 classes.
 7. How:
 1. Replaces legacy 105mm, 122mm, and 130mm guns with 155mm/45-calibre and
 155mm/52-calibre howitzers.
 2. Combines indigenous designs (Dhanush, ATAGS) with licence-built (K9 Vajra-T)
 and imported (M777) platforms.
 3. Upgrades existing 130mm M-46 guns to 155mm/45-calibre Sharang howitzers as
 an interim measure.
 8. Why: Standardising on 155mm simplifies ammunition logistics and increases range and
 lethality across both mountain and plains sectors.
 9. Compare with the US: The US Army already standardised on 155mm for medium/
 heavy tube artillery decades ago, whereas India still operates thousands of 105mm,
 122mm, and 130mm tubes.
 10. Confidence: Confirmed
 11. As of: November 2026

 2. Area: Guns, Howitzers and Mortars
 3. Section: Equipment
 4. Force: Indian Army
 5. Echelon: Regiment / Brigade
 6. What: ATAGS is an indigenous 155mm/52-calibre towed howitzer developed by DRDO
 with Bharat Forge and Tata Advanced Systems, with 307 guns on order.
 7. How:
 1. Uses an all-electric drive for gun laying, loading, and ramming to reduce

 Automatic Gun Alignment and Positioning System (AGAPS).
 8. Why: ATAGS gives Indian towed regiments 52-calibre range and high burst rates of fire
 without foreign supply-chain dependencies.
 9. Compare with the US: ATAGS uses a 52-calibre barrel for longer range than the US
 Army's 39-calibre M777A2, at the cost of significantly greater weight.

 concentrations, gun positions, and logistics hubs.
 9. Compare with the US: Pinaka bridges the gap between unguided area rockets and US
 HIMARS/GMLRS, with guided Pinaka variants moving toward precision strike.

Entry 11

 1. Title: Swathi Weapon Locating Radar (WLR)
 2. Area: Target Acquisition
 3. Section: Equipment
 4. Force: Indian Army
 5. Echelon: Regiment / Brigade, Division
 6. What: Swathi is an indigenous C-band phased-array counter-battery radar developed by
 DRDO's LRDE and built by Bharat Electronics Limited (BEL).
 7. How:
 1. Detects and tracks incoming 81mm+ mortars (2–20 km), 105mm+ artillery shells
 (2–30 km), and 120mm+ unguided rockets (4–48 km).
 2. Tracks up to 7 targets simultaneously and stores up to 99 weapon locations on a
 100 × 100 km digital map.
 3. Operates in two modes: Weapon Location Mode for enemy guns and Direction of
 Own Artillery Fire (DOOAF) Mode to adjust friendly fire.
 4. Fielded in Plains (Tatra 8×8, 43 in service) and Mountains Mk2 (Tatra 6×6, 6 in
 service) variants.
 8. Why: Swathi closed the counter-battery gap exposed during the 1999 Kargil War, where
 enemy artillery caused the majority of Indian casualties.
 9. Compare with the US: Performs the same counter-fire locating mission as the US
 Army's AN/TPQ-53 radar, with a dedicated lighter variant for Himalayan terrain.
 10. Confidence: Confirmed
 11. As of: November 2026

Entry 18

 1. Title: Kargil War Massed Fires and Counter-Battery Lessons
 2. Area: Counter-Fire and Force Protection
 3. Section: Lessons
 4. Force: Indian Army
 5. Echelon: Division, Corps / Army
 6. What: The 1999 Kargil conflict proved that massed 155mm medium artillery was
 decisive in mountain assaults, while exposing a critical lack of weapon-locating radars.
 7. How:
 1. Indian forces deployed up to 120 guns to support single battalion attacks, such
 as at Tololing, establishing a norm of ~100 tubes per mountain battalion assault.
 2. Because India lacked modern fire-finding radars, enemy artillery caused roughly
 80% of Indian casualties.
 3. These lessons directly drove the Field Artillery Rationalisation Plan (155mm
 standardisation) and the indigenous Swathi WLR programme.
 8. Why: Kargil remains the foundational operational reference for Indian mountain fire-
 support density and counter-battery investment.
 9. Compare with the US: While the US Army shifted toward precision-guided munitions for
 low-density counter-insurgency in the 2000s, India retained a doctrine of massed 155mm
 volume fires.

PART D: KILL CHAIN

 ● Name: Sensor-to-Shooter Counter-Fire and Area Strike
 ● Nodes: Sensor (Swathi WLR, Forward Observers, UAVs) → Command / Fire Direction
 Node (Project Shakti ACCCS) → Shooter (Pinaka MBRL / ATAGS / K9 Vajra-T Battery)
 → Target
 1. Find: Swathi WLR detects incoming enemy artillery trajectories (or observers/drones
 locate enemy troop concentrations) and plots coordinates on a digital map. (Supporting
 entry: Swathi Weapon Locating Radar (WLR))
 2. Report: Target coordinates are transmitted digitally from the sensor to the Artillery
 Combat Command and Control System (Project Shakti). (Supporting entry: Project
 Shakti (ACCCS) Digital Fire Control)
 3. Decide & Task: The command post evaluates target priority via Shakti tactical fire
 control and assigns the mission to an available firing battery (such as Pinaka or ATAGS).
 (Supporting entry: Project Shakti (ACCCS) Digital Fire Control)
 4. Fire: The firing battery computes technical firing data (via onboard ballistic computers/
 AGAPS) and executes the fire mission—for Pinaka, 72 rockets across six launchers in
 under 44 seconds (Reported, India Defence Wire, 24 September 2026). (Supporting
 entry: Pinaka Multi-Barrel Rocket Launcher (MBRL))
 5. Move: Immediately after completing the salvo, the launchers or self-propelled guns
 displace to avoid enemy counter-battery fire ("shoot and scoot"). (Supporting entry:
 Shoot-and-Scoot Counter-Fire Survivability)
 6. Assess: Swathi WLR (in DOOAF mode), observers, or drones verify round impact and
 battle damage to determine whether re-engagement is required. (Supporting entry:
 Swathi Weapon Locating Radar (WLR))
