India Artillery and Fire Support
Reference: Operation Sindoor,
Gap-Filling Entries and Source
Verification

This supplement adds ten structured entries and one evidence verification update to the India
artillery and fire support reference, using open sources current to November 2026. Task A
delivers three entries on the May 2025 India–Pakistan clash (Operation Sindoor), separating
confirmed deployments from unverified claims by both governments. Task B fills the four thin
reference areas—Ammunition and Effects, Target Acquisition, Digital Fires and Targeting, and
Sustainment—with seven non-overlapping entries. Task C replaces assessment in Why India Is
Buying Mobility and Loitering Munitions with official Ministry of Defence (MoD) contracts and
policy releases from 2023 to 2026, followed by a Corrections section covering dated items in
existing entries.
Two evidentiary caveats apply throughout. First, wartime battle damage assessments,
shootdown tallies and counter-battery losses from May 2025 remain disputed between New
Delhi and Islamabad; every uncorroborated official or press claim is marked Reported:. Second,
India does not publish unit-level artillery establishments, post-2017 War Wastage Reserve
holdings or May 2025 ammunition expenditure; those omissions are stated plainly as not found
in open sources.
Task A: The May 2025 India–Pakistan Clash (Operation
Sindoor)

Operation Sindoor (7–10 May 2025) was the first India–Pakistan crisis in which both sides
combined cross-Line of Control (LoC) precision artillery, one-way attack drones, cruise missiles
and conventionally armed short-range ballistic missiles inside a four-day escalation cycle. The
phased fires timeline below maps the action-reaction sequence and separates confirmed
system employment from contested claims before the three reference entries examine LoC
artillery, unmanned and missile duels, and post-conflict lessons.

Entry A1

 1. Title: Operation Sindoor: Air-Mobilised Artillery and LoC Counter-Battery
 2. Area: Counter-Fire and Force Protection
 3. Section: Lessons
 4. Force: Indian Army (Regiment of Artillery and Infantry)
 5. Echelon: Regiment
 6. What: During Operation Sindoor (7–10 May 2025), Indian artillery executed the Army's
 first air-mobilised inter-command gun deployment and fired precision 155mm strikes
 across the Line of Control (LoC). Heavy LoC artillery and mortar exchanges followed the
 initial strikes and caused a major share of total conflict casualties on both sides.
7. How:
 1. Reported: 302 Medium Regiment, led by Col Koshank Lamba (awarded the Vir
 Chakra), air-mobilised M777 155mm ultra-light howitzers underslung from Indian Air
 Force CH-47 Chinook and Mi-17 V5 helicopters with ammunition, communications,
 target acquisition gear and force-protection stores.
 2. Reported: On 7 May 2025, Indian Army guns and loitering munitions hit seven of
 the nine designated militant sites. 302 Medium Regiment fired US-made M982
 Excalibur guided shells, while 1988 (Independent) Medium Battery (under Lt Col
 Sushil Bisht, Vir Chakra) deployed at night using current satellite imagery.
 3. Reported: Both artillery units practised rapid extrication and shoot-and-scoot drills
 under Pakistani counter-bombardment; Lt Col Bisht's battery redeployed under
 shelling to strike a second target.
 4. Reported: Indian state media and open-source accounts reported Pakistani artillery
 and mortar fire across Poonch, Rajouri, Kupwara, Baramulla, Uri and Akhnoor,
 causing civilian deaths in Poonch. Infantry mortar detachments, including a 4th
 Battalion Dogra Regiment platoon under Naib Subedar Satish Kumar (Vir Chakra),
 conducted counter-mortar fire that suppressed enemy fire and destroyed two
 surveillance cameras without friendly losses.
 5. Reported: Pakistani academic and press accounts stated Pakistan accelerated LoC
 artillery and drone fire in response, alleged Indian strikes killed at least 31 people
 and injured 71 (citing Reuters), and claimed damage to civilian areas and the
 Neelum–Jhelum Hydropower Plant. Not found in open sources: direct
 Inter-Services Public Relations (ISPR) LoC counter-battery logs, verified tactical
 battle damage or total round expenditure on either side.
8. Why: Air-mobilising lightweight 155mm howitzers let India mass precision indirect fire in
 Northern Command without telegraphing road convoys. Pairing guided shells with night
 deployment and swift extrication reduced exposure to Pakistani counter-battery fire in
 observed mountain sectors.
9. Compare with the US: The US Army similarly uses CH-47 helicopters to air-assault
 M777A2 howitzers firing M982A1 Excalibur shells, though US units rely on continuous
 brigade-to-company digital fire-control links.
10. Confidence: Reported (gallantry citations and unit roles come from established Indian
 press; official MoD releases confirm Army LoC retaliation and nine strikes without naming
 gun units).

Entry A2

 1. Title: Operation Sindoor: Loitering Munitions, Drones and Missile Duels
 2. Area: Loitering Munitions and Armed Drones
 3. Section: Lessons
 4. Force: Tri-Services (Indian Army, Indian Air Force and Indian Navy)
 5. Echelon: Theatre
 6. What: Operation Sindoor was the first India–Pakistan conflict in which both sides
 conducted offensive drone warfare, cruise missile strikes and conventionally armed
 short-range ballistic missile (SRBM) launches. Indian loitering munitions targeted enemy
 air defences, while a layered air-defence and counter-UAS grid defeated Pakistani drone
 and missile salvos.
 7. How:
 1. Indian forces fired BrahMos and SCALP-EG cruise missiles alongside loitering
 munitions from Indian territory without crossing the border. Reported: Defence
 reporting identified Israeli-origin Harop and Bengaluru-assembled SkyStriker
 loitering munitions; official Indian releases claimed initial strikes finished in 23
 minutes, neutralised an air-defence system at Lahore on 8 May and struck
 Pakistani airbases including Nur Khan (Chaklala), Sargodha, Rafiqui, Rahimyar
 Khan, Jacobabad, Sukkur and Bholari.
 2. Reported: On 7–8 May, India stated Pakistan launched drones—including
 Turkish-origin Yiha-III kamikaze drones, whose debris was recovered—and missiles
 toward 15 northern and western Indian locations. Indian media cited roughly 400
 drones across 36 locations on 8–9 May, though Stimson noted the true scale
 remained unclear.
 3. Under Operation Bunyanum Marsoos on 10 May, Pakistan officially confirmed
 launching conventionally armed Fatah-I and Fatah-II SRBMs; India also reported
 recovering Hatf-I debris and intercepting Pakistani drones aimed at airfields and
 Army ammunition depots.
 4. India's Integrated Air Command and Control System (IACCS) linked Army
 Akashteer and Navy Trigun networks across four tiers: counter-UAS, shoulder-fired
 missiles, upgraded low-level air-defence (LLAD) guns firing air-burst rounds, and
 surface-to-air missiles (Akash, Pechora, OSA-AK and S-400), intercepting incoming
 drones and a Fatah-II over Sirsa. Reported: Indian analysts also credited the
 indigenous D-4 counter-drone system with jamming and laser interdiction.
 5. Reported: Competing claims remain unresolved. India acknowledged limited drone
 damage at Udhampur, Pathankot, Adampur and Bhuj and zero pilot losses while
 declining to confirm aircraft losses; foreign government officials told international
 media at least one or two Indian aircraft were downed. IAF Chief Amar Preet Singh
 claimed Akash downed five Pakistani fighters, which Stimson assessed as
 unsupported by public evidence. Pakistan claimed major damage to 15 Indian
 airbases and destruction of an S-400 component at Adampur without releasing
 public imagery, while Pakistani channels (Geo, Dunya, ARY) claimed 25 to 32
 Indian drones shot down.
 8. Why: Dense surface-to-air missile envelopes kept manned aircraft inside their own
 airspace, pushing deep strike and SEAD roles onto standoff missiles and loitering
 munitions. Upgraded legacy air-defence guns offered a lower-cost layer against small
 drones than missile interceptors.
 9. Compare with the US: The US Army similarly integrates short-range air defence and
 counter-UAS under its Integrated Battle Command System (IBCS), but does not field
 dedicated artillery regiments built around long-range loitering munitions such as Harop.
 10. Confidence: Confirmed (for core weapon families deployed and official Indian and
 Pakistani acknowledgements; all unverified shootdowns, swarm counts and battle
 damage assessments are marked Reported:).

Entry A3

 1. Title: Post-Sindoor Fires Lessons: Force Restructuring, C4I and Procurement
 2. Area: Liaison and Fire Support Integration
 3. Section: Lessons
 4. Force: Indian Army and Integrated Defence Staff
 5. Echelon: Theatre
 6. What: Following Operation Sindoor, Indian military leaders accelerated force restructuring
 around organic drone and loitering-munition units, proposed a dedicated
 rocket-cum-missile force, and highlighted C4I integration and acquisition speed as
 primary constraints.
7. How:
 1. Official MoD releases confirmed deployment of newly created Rudra brigades,
 Bhairav battalions and Divyastra artillery batteries along the Northern Front by
 December 2025, followed by public unveiling of Shaktibaan and Divyastra
 unmanned combat support elements in January 2026. Reported: Chief of the Army
 Staff Gen Upendra Dwivedi announced these formations alongside Ashni infantry
 drone platoons on 26 July 2025.
 2. Chief of Defence Staff Gen Anil Chauhan listed post-Sindoor unmanned priorities:
 modular UAV architectures, startup and DRDO test beds, stealth UAVs,
 manned-unmanned teaming, swarm drones, AI integration and directed-energy
 weapons.
 3. Reported: Ahead of Army Day in January 2026, Gen Dwivedi stated India needed a
 dedicated rocket-cum-missile force. Prime Minister Narendra Modi also announced
 Mission Sudarshan Chakra after Operation Sindoor, which ORF reports aims for
 nationwide layered air and missile defence by 2035.
 4. To replenish and harden precision stocks, Washington cleared a possible sale of
 216 M982A1 Excalibur shells in November 2025, and India's Defence Acquisition
 Council (DAC) approved Artillery Regiment loitering munitions and long-range
 guided Pinaka rockets on 29 December 2025. Reported: ORF noted late-2025
 emergency procurement of indigenous drones worth over ₹5,000 crore requiring
 testing under "Sindoor-like" jamming conditions.
 5. Assessments by Carnegie, ORF and CENJOWS identified four systemic gaps
 exposed by the clash: slow regular procurement forcing reliance on emergency
 powers, tactical datalink constraints across mixed platforms, persistent ISR and
 intelligence-fusion limits, and weak strategic communication during rapid escalation.
8. Why: Operation Sindoor showed that India's strike hardware had outpaced its joint
 tactical networks and standard procurement timelines. Embedding drones, jamming
 resilience and guided replenishment inside artillery and combined-arms formations aims
 to compress the sensor-to-shooter cycle before the next crisis.
9. Compare with the US: The US Army similarly combines long-range precision fires,
 intelligence and air defence in theatre-level Multi-Domain Task Forces (MDTFs), whereas
 India is grafting drone-artillery units onto existing corps and brigades while theatre
 commands remain incomplete.
10. Confidence: Confirmed (for official MoD restructuring deployments, CDS priorities, DAC
 approvals and DSCA Excalibur notification; press and think-tank items are marked
 Reported: or attributed as assessments).
11. As of: November 2026

Ammunition and Effects

Entry B1

 1. Title: M982A1 Excalibur: Precision-Guided 155mm Projectiles
 2. Area: Ammunition and Effects
 3. Section: Equipment
 4. Force: Indian Army (Regiment of Artillery)
 5. Echelon: Brigade
 6. What: India fields US-made M982/M982A1 Excalibur GPS-guided 155mm shells for
 first-round precision engagement against point targets along the LoC and Line of Actual
 Control. After Operation Sindoor, Washington cleared a follow-on Foreign Military Sale of
 up to 216 M982A1 rounds in November 2025.
 7. How:
 1. Reported: India first procured Excalibur projectiles in 2019 for use with its M777
 ultra-light howitzers. Not found in open sources: total Indian Excalibur holdings prior
 to November 2025 or exact round counts fired in May 2025.
 2. Reported: Open technical references list the projectile at 48 kg and 1.0 m long, with
 deployable glide fins reaching roughly 40 km from 39-calibre guns (such as M777)
 and up to 50 km from 52-calibre howitzers. Circular error probable (CEP) is cited as
 4 m in open technical specifications, whereas Indian press reporting cites accuracy
 down to 2 m.
 3. Reported: Beyond the M777, Indian press accounts state Excalibur is compatible
 with the Bofors FH-77B, Dhanush (45-calibre), ATAGS (52-calibre) and K9 Vajra-T
 (52-calibre).
 4. On 19 November 2025, the US Defense Security Cooperation Agency (DSCA)
 notified Congress (Transmittal No. 25-107) of a possible sale of up to 216 M982A1
 tactical projectiles for an estimated $47.1 million, including Portable Electronic Fire
 Control Systems (PEFCS) with Improved Platform Integration Kits (iPIK), primers,
 propellant charges, technical data and repair-and-return services.
 5. Reported: Open-source accounts of Ukraine combat use noted that Russian
 electronic warfare cut Excalibur hit efficiency from 70% to 6% within six weeks,
 illustrating GPS-jamming risk against modern adversaries.
 8. Why: Guided shells let brigades destroy hardened bunkers or point targets with single
 rounds rather than multi-battery barrages, cutting helicopter lift weight in mountains and
 limiting collateral damage. High cost—roughly
 218, 000𝑝𝑒𝑟𝑟𝑜𝑢𝑛𝑑𝑝𝑎𝑐𝑘𝑎𝑔𝑒𝑑𝑖𝑛𝐼𝑛𝑑𝑖𝑎'𝑠2025𝐹𝑀𝑆𝑛𝑜𝑡𝑖𝑓𝑖𝑐𝑎𝑡𝑖𝑜𝑛(47.1 million divided across
 216 rounds plus fire-control kits and support; FY2021 US budget data reported $112,800
 per shell)—restricts employment to priority targets.
 9. Compare with the US: India buys the same M982A1 Excalibur round and PEFCS
 fire-control hardware used by US Army and Marine Corps M777A2 batteries, though US
 forces hold deeper stocks and supplement Excalibur with cheaper Precision Guidance
 Kits (PGK).
 10. Confidence: Confirmed (for the November 2025 DSCA sale quantity, estimated cost and
 ancillary kits; technical range/CEP figures and May 2025 combat usage are marked
 Reported:).

Entry B2

 1. Title: Bi-Modular Charge System, Electronic Fuzes and Guided Pinaka Munitions
 2. Area: Ammunition and Effects
 3. Section: Equipment
 4. Force: Indian Army (Regiment of Artillery)
 5. Echelon: Regiment
 6. What: India is modernising artillery propellants, fuzes and rocket warheads by shifting
 from bag charges to Bi-Modular Charge Systems (BMCS), contracting domestic electronic
 fuzes, and fielding extended-range, guided and area-denial rockets for Pinaka.
7. How:
 1. The Cabinet Committee on Security sanctioned a Nalanda, Bihar factory in
 November 2001 (estimated cost ₹941.13 crore) to make 200,000 BMCS units
 annually for 155mm guns, but Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) audits in
 2010 and 2016 documented procedural lapses, corruption controversies over the
 Israel Military Industries plant contract and prolonged import reliance.
 2. Reported: After tie-ups with two foreign OEMs and an initial DRDO project
 struggled, indigenous BMCS production progressed via state factories and private
 Indian firms including Solar Industries. Official MoD releases confirm modernised
 155mm guns such as Dhanush fire both legacy charge bags and BMCS.
 3. To resolve artillery fuze shortfalls noted in earlier analyses, the MoD signed a
 10-year, ₹5,336 crore contract with Bharat Electronics Limited (BEL) in 2023 for
 electronic fuzes.
 4. For rocket artillery, DRDO and Indian industry developed Enhanced Pinaka,
 Extended Range Pinaka (Pinaka-ER), Area Denial Munition (ADM) Type-1
 (dual-purpose improved conventional munition, DPICM) contracted with Economic
 Explosive Limited, and Guided Pinaka rockets.
 5. On 29 December 2025, the DAC accorded Acceptance of Necessity for Long
 Range Guided Rocket Ammunition for the Pinaka Multiple Launch Rocket System.
 Not found in open sources: exact warhead weights, submunition counts per ADM
 rocket or unit prices.
8. Why: Standardising on rigid modular charges raises rate of fire, simplifies automatic
 loading on 155mm/52-calibre guns and lowers barrel wear, while domestic electronic
 fuzes and guided Pinaka rockets ease foreign supply bottlenecks.
9. Compare with the US: The US Army fielded its Modular Artillery Charge System (MACS)
 and Guided Multiple Launch Rocket System (GMLRS) decades earlier, whereas India is
 still scaling domestic BMCS output and transitioning Pinaka from unguided area rockets
 to guided munitions.
10. Confidence: Confirmed (for CAG Nalanda audit findings, Dhanush BMCS compatibility,
 BEL fuze contract, Pinaka ADM Type-1 contract and December 2025 DAC guided rocket
 approval; private BMCS lines are marked Reported:).
11. As of: November 2026

Target Acquisition

Entry B3

 1. Title: Surveillance and Target Acquisition Units: Radars, LORROS and UAVs
 2. Area: Target Acquisition
 3. Section: Forces
 4. Force: Indian Army (Regiment of Artillery)
 5. Echelon: Regiment
 6. What: Beyond Swathi weapon-locating radars, the Regiment of Artillery operates
 Surveillance and Target Acquisition (SATA) regiments and independent SATA batteries as
 the Army's primary organic target-acquisition force. These units combine battlefield
 surveillance radars, long-range electro-optical sensors and UAVs to locate targets and
 direct fire.
 7. How:
 1. Official MoD honours lists confirm active SATA regiments (such as 21 SATA
 Regiment and 203 SATA Regiment) and independent SATA batteries (such as 617,
 627 and 630 SATA Batteries) within the Regiment of Artillery. The 2023 MoD Year
 End Review confirmed SATA unit reorganisation alongside operationalisation of
 Heron Mk II remotely piloted aircraft.
 2. Reported: Centre for Land Warfare Studies (CLAWS) analysis notes SATA ground
 surveillance uses three tiers of Battlefield Surveillance Radars (BFSR)—short-,
 medium- and long-range—to detect moving personnel and vehicles.
 3. Reported: Day-and-night electro-optical observation relies on the Long Range
 Reconnaissance and Observation System (LORROS), fielded for roughly two
 decades and identified by CLAWS as needing a longer-range, higher-resolution
 successor.
 4. Aerial target acquisition pairs Heron medium-altitude long-endurance UAVs with
 newer tactical aircraft; in January 2026, PIB confirmed fielding of ZOLT, an
 indigenous tactical hybrid UAV employed specifically to direct artillery fire, alongside
 swarm and tethered surveillance drones.
 5. Not found in open sources: personnel establishments, exact sensor counts per
 SATA regiment or current operational availability rates.
 8. Why: Grouping radars, electro-optical devices and UAVs inside artillery-controlled SATA
 formations lets corps and divisional headquarters observe depth areas continuously and
 pass coordinates directly to gun and rocket units.
 9. Compare with the US: The US Army places target-acquisition platoons inside field
 artillery battalions and relies on aviation-branch Shadow and Gray Eagle UAS rather than
 maintaining standalone artillery SATA regiments.
 10. Confidence: Confirmed (for SATA unit designations, ongoing reorganisation, Heron Mk II
 operationalisation and ZOLT artillery-direction UAV; BFSR tiers and LORROS
 replacement requirements are marked Reported:).

Digital Fires and Targeting

The two entries below examine opposite sides of India's digital fires architecture: the 2025
fielding of the SANJAY Battlefield Surveillance System at brigade-to-corps echelons, and the
tactical C4I void below brigade level left by programme cancellations and communications
delays. The architectural schematic below contrasts these operational upper networks against
the broken below-brigade seam.

Entry B4

 1. Title: SANJAY: India’s Automated Battlefield Surveillance System
 2. Area: Digital Fires and Targeting
 3. Section: Equipment
 4. Force: Indian Army
 5. Echelon: Corps
 6. What: SANJAY is the Indian Army's automated Battlefield Surveillance System (BSS),
 jointly developed with Bharat Electronics Limited (BEL) for ₹2,402 crore and flagged off on
 24 January 2025. It fuses ground and aerial sensor feeds into a single Common
 Surveillance Picture for field formations.
 7. How:
 1. Developed indigenously under the "Buy (Indian)" category, SANJAY automates
 intake from all ground and aerial battlefield sensors.
 2. Built-in software checks report veracity, strips duplicate tracks and fuses inputs
 through analytics into a unified Common Surveillance Picture.
 3. Surveillance data travels over a secured Army Data Network and Satellite
 Communication Network.
 4. A Centralised Web Application delivers real-time inputs to Command Headquarters,
 Army Headquarters and the Indian Army Decision Support System.
 5. Induction across all operational brigades, divisions and corps was scheduled in
 three phases between March and October 2025. Not found in open sources: public
 confirmation of phase-three completion or technical interface details linking
 SANJAY directly to the Shakti ACCCS fire-control network.
 8. Why: Automated multi-sensor deconfliction replaces slow manual plotting across
 Himalayan and western borders, accelerating situational awareness for conventional and
 sub-conventional operations.
 9. Compare with the US: SANJAY performs an operational sensor-fusion role comparable
 to the US Army's Distributed Common Ground System–Army (DCGS-A) and TITAN
 intelligence architectures, though SANJAY stops at brigade headquarters rather than
 extending to manoeuvre companies.
 10. Confidence: Confirmed (official MoD PIB release specifying cost, developer, architecture

Entry B5

 1. Title: The C4I and Kill-Chain Gap: BMS Cancellation and TCS Delays
 2. Area: Digital Fires and Targeting
 3. Section: Lessons
 4. Force: Indian Army and Tri-Services
 5. Echelon: Corps
 6. What: India's precision strike platforms have outpaced its tactical command-and-control
 networks, creating what defence analysts call an "effect-layer inversion". Although
 tri-service air-defence networking operated coherently during Operation Sindoor,
 programme cancellations and communications delays leave a digital gap below brigade
 level and across service seams.
 7. How:
 1. Between FY 2019–20 and FY 2024–25, Indian defence capital expenditure rose
 52% to ₹1.72 lakh crore, funding long-range systems such as extended-range
 Pinaka, Pralay, BrahMos, Rafale and MQ-9B drones.
 2. In July 2018, the Army foreclosed the Battlefield Management System (BMS) after
 seven years of development and an estimated ₹5,000 crore spent on prototypes;
 BMS was meant to connect battalion headquarters digitally to companies and
 platoons, and no follow-on system was fielded seven years later.
 3. The Tactical Communication System (TCS), intended to replace legacy radios with
 encrypted digital links from corps down to section level, remained unfielded after 25
 years of development as of July 2026.
 4. Kill-chain latency therefore accumulates in processing and manual hierarchical
 approval chains rather than detection or flight time, constraining weapons reaching
 300–400 km.
 5. Operation Sindoor showed a sharp split: the air-defence triad (IAF IACCS, Army
 Akashteer contracted with BEL for ₹2,400 crore in 2023, and Navy Trigun)
 performed coherently, whereas post-conflict reviews flagged tactical datalink limits
 on the IAF's mixed fleet and persistent inter-service seams.
 8. Why: Long-range guns and missiles lose responsiveness against moving targets when
 frontline observers and joint headquarters cannot pass verified coordinates digitally
 across echelons and services. Network integration, rather than platform acquisition, has
 emerged as a primary constraint on Indian joint fires.
 9. Compare with the US: Whereas the US military is linking tactical manoeuvre units,
 artillery fire direction and joint sensors under Combined Joint All-Domain Command and
 Control (CJADC2), India retains strong single-service networks separated by
 below-brigade and inter-service seams.
 10. Confidence: Analysis (based on Observer Research Foundation and CENJOWS studies
 plus official PIB contract records for Akashteer).

Sustainment

Sustaining massed artillery and guided munitions requires adequate War Wastage Reserves
(WWR), dependable factory output and modernised depot accounting. The scorecard below
presents the baseline quantitative findings from the 2015 and 2017 Comptroller and Auditor
General (CAG) audits—the latest public official stockpile reviews—before the two Sustainment
entries examine reserve governance, Ordnance Factory corporatisation and forward
maintenance.

Entry B6

 1. Title: War Wastage Reserves and Ammunition Stockpile Management
2. Area: Sustainment
3. Section: Lessons
4. Force: Indian Army
5. Echelon: Corps
6. What: The Indian Army sizes its War Wastage Reserve (WWR) for 40 days of intense
 two-front combat, yet CAG audits exposed chronic shortfalls in high-calibre artillery and
 tank rounds alongside depot bottlenecks. Emergency procurement powers later helped
 rebuild reserves ahead of Operation Sindoor.
7. How:
 1. WWR scales cover 30 days intense (I) and 30 days normal (N) combat—calculated
 as 40 (I) days at one intense day per three normal days—yet Army Headquarters
 indented against a lower Minimum Acceptable Risk Level (MARL) of 20 (I) days
 (50% of WWR).
 2. CAG Report No. 19 of 2015 (covering 2008–09 to 2012–13) found ammunition
 types in the critical under-10-day category rose from 15% in March 2009 to 50% in
 March 2013, while critical high-calibre shortages reached up to 84%.
 3. The Ordnance Factory Board (OFB), supplying 80–90% of Army ammunition,
 missed first Roll-on Indent targets across up to 73% of types; none of 17 import
 cases sanctioned in July 2013 reached contract by December 2014.
 4. CAG inspections recorded ₹8,119 crore of depot stock unavailable for issue: ₹3,578
 crore segregated after accidents awaiting sentencing, ₹2,109 crore awaiting major
 repair, ₹1,618 crore rejected for quality defects and ₹814 crore unserviceable within
 shelf life, alongside a 10-year delay in depot computerisation.
 5. Reported: Press summaries of a July 2017 CAG follow-up found no major WWR
 improvement since 2013, with 61 of 152 critical types below 10 days and 77–88%
 of training ammunition below five days. Subsequent emergency procurement
 powers cushioned readiness before Operation Sindoor. Not found in open sources:
 post-2017 WWR percentages or May 2025 expenditure.
8. Why: High-intensity border clashes burn through artillery magazines quickly; weak quality
 assurance, slow import contracting and paper-bound depots can starve gun regiments
 even when budgets exist.
9. Compare with the US: The US Army also faced 155mm war-reserve strain during the
 Ukraine war, but tracks munitions across networked enterprise logistics rather than
 uncomputerised depots.
10. Confidence: Confirmed (for 2015 CAG audit findings and WWR definitions; 2017 CAG
 press summaries and pre-Sindoor stocking assessments are marked Reported:).

Entry B7

 1. Title: Corporatised Munitions Supply and Forward Maintenance
 2. Area: Sustainment
 3. Section: Forces
 4. Force: Ministry of Defence and Indian Army (Corps of Electronics and Mechanical
 Engineers)
 5. Echelon: Theatre
 6. What: India dissolved the Ordnance Factory Board into seven corporate Defence Public
 Sector Undertakings (DPSUs) in 2021 to fix supply shortfalls, while pairing foreign
 life-cycle support for imported guns with forward-area drone maintenance vehicles.
 7. How:
 1. Following Cabinet approval on 16 June 2021, the MoD converted 41 state ordnance
 factories into seven DPSUs after 30 September 2021, supported by an ₹8,745
 crore capital expenditure fund running from FY 2021–22 through FY 2026–27.
 2. Artillery supply split chiefly between two new DPSUs: Munitions India Limited
 (Pune, manufacturing ammunition and explosives at plants including Ammunition
 Factory Khadki and Cordite Factory Aruvankadu) and Advanced Weapons and
 Equipment India Limited (Kanpur, manufacturing weapons including Gun Carriage

 US-origin fires gear; arms-trade tracking records a May 2026 US Congressional
 notification for $230 million in M777A2 ultra-light howitzer support, alongside
 repair-and-return services confirmed in the November 2025 Excalibur DSCA notice.
 4. At the tactical edge, the Corps of Electronics and Mechanical Engineers (EME)
 introduced the Drone Shakti Lorry—displayed at the January 2026 Republic Day
 parade—to support Army drone operations in forward areas.
 5. Not found in open sources: unit-level barrel replacement rates, M777A2 spares
 availability or post-2021 Munitions India Limited on-time delivery metrics for 155mm
 shells.
 8. Why: Splitting state factories into product-focused corporations with dedicated capex
 aims to improve quality control and accountability, while OEM support deals and mobile
 EME workshops keep mixed imported and unmanned fleets serviceable near the front.
 9. Compare with the US: The US Army sustains cannon and munitions mainly through
 government-owned, contractor-operated (GOCO) plants and organic brigade support
 battalions, whereas India relies on newly corporatised state DPSUs supplemented by
 private domestic vendors and foreign OEM support packages.

Task C: Evidence Strengthening — Why India Is
Buying Mobility and Loitering Munitions

The existing entry Why India Is Buying Mobility and Loitering Munitions previously rested on
assessment without linked primary sources. Official Ministry of Defence press releases from
2023 to 2026 now confirm both halves of the thesis with signed domestic contracts, explicit
terrain rationales and fielded formations, while defence press and think-tank studies document
the external war lessons shaping those buys.
The chart below aggregates six confirmed domestic MoD contracts signed between 2023 and
August 2026—exceeding ₹20,300 crore in total—to show how mobility and loitering-munition
buys sit alongside major outlays for fuzes, surveillance fusion and air-defence networking.

Confirmed Official Evidence (Mobility)

 1. All-terrain and high-altitude tracked mobility: On 20 December 2024, the MoD signed
 a ₹7,628.70 crore contract with Larsen & Toubro for follow-on 155mm/52-calibre K9
 VAJRA-T self-propelled tracked guns. The PIB release states the official rationale directly:
 cross-country mobility, deeper precision strikes, higher rate of fire and full operational
 capability across all terrains, including sub-zero temperatures in high-altitude areas.
 2. High-mobility wheeled mounts and gun tractors: The 2023 MoD Year End Review
 confirmed March 2023 DAC approval for 155mm/52-calibre ATAGS alongside High
 Mobility and Gun Towing Vehicles. On 25 January 2026, PIB confirmed that Shaktibaan
 and Divyastra unmanned elements are mounted on specialised High Mobility Vehicles
 (HMV 6x6), while a 2026 PIB release also lists the Mounted Gun System (MGS).
Confirmed Official Evidence (Loitering Munitions)

 1. Combat validation and operational role: PIB's 14 May 2025 Operation Sindoor release
 confirmed that loitering munitions searched, hovered over target areas and struck
 high-value radar and missile sites, citing Bengaluru-based Alpha Design Technologies'
 partnership with Israel's Elbit Systems on SkyStriker. Chief of Defence Staff Gen Anil
 Chauhan officially stated in 2025 that Operation Sindoor demonstrated the operational
 effectiveness and strategic value of indigenous UAVs and counter-UAS.
 2. Dedicated formations and fielded inventory: The 2025 MoD Year End Review
 confirmed Northern Front deployment of Divyastra batteries and December 2025 DAC
 approval of a Loiter Munition System for Artillery Regiments. PIB's 25 January 2026
 parade release confirmed that Shaktibaan and Divyastra operate HAROP, Mini HARPY,
 Peacekeeper, ATS (Extended Range), ATS (Medium Range) and SKY STRIKER loitering
 munitions plus long-range drones exceeding 1,000 km.
 3. Contracted domestic acquisition: On 14 August 2026, the MoD signed ₹1,577 crore
 contracts with Tata Advanced Systems Limited (TASL) and Nibe Pvt Ltd for Loiter
 Munition Systems for the Indian Army.

Press-Reported Doctrinal Drivers

 ● Reported: Director General of Artillery Lt Gen Adosh Kumar stated in September 2024
 that the Army was conducting trials to acquire long-range loitering munitions.
 ● Reported: Economic Times Defence (November 2026), Carnegie Endowment (October
 2025) and Observer Research Foundation (March 2026) report that lessons from the
 Russia–Ukraine war, the Armenia–Azerbaijan conflict and Operation Sindoor pushed the
 Army toward shoot-and-scoot mobility, jamming-resilient domestic drones (over ₹5,000
 crore in late-2025 emergency procurement) and integrated gun-drone strike architectures.
