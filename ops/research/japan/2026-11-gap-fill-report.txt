Japan Artillery and Fire Support:
Reference Additions, Operational
Lessons, Equipment Baselines, and
Corpus Corrections

This reference supplement expands and audits the 17-entry Japan artillery and fire support
learning corpus for military professionals, incorporating open-source official documentation,
defense institute analyses, and established defense reporting current through November 2026.
It delivers eight new doctrinal and technical entries across the four under-documented thematic
areas—Liaison and Fire Support Integration, Loitering Munitions and Armed Drones, Target
Acquisition, and Counter-Fire and Force Protection—followed by two operational
lessons-learned syntheses drawn from the war in Ukraine and multilateral Indo-Pacific
exercises. It also establishes verified technical baselines for the legacy tracked Type 96 120mm
self-propelled mortar and Type 99 155mm self-propelled howitzer, separating official Japan
Ministry of Defense (MOD) figures from secondary reported values and explicitly marking
unverified parameters.
Between March 2025 and October 2026, Japan Ground Self-Defense Force (JGSDF) fires
crossed a structural inflection point that renders portions of the existing 17-entry reference
outdated. Key milestones include the permanent activation of the JSDF Joint Operations
Command (JJOC) in March 2025, the FY2025 fielding of the ground-launched Type 25
Surface-to-Ship Guided Missile (25SSM) and Type 25 Hyper Velocity Gliding Projectile (HVGP),
the FY2026 budget allocation of approximately ¥100.1 billion for the Synchronized, Hybrid,
Integrated and Enhanced Littoral Defense (SHIELD) unmanned architecture, and the integration
of electronic warfare units and dispersed command posts into domestic missile live-fire
exercises. Where official MOD sources withhold sensitive performance metrics—notably
maximum firing ranges, sustained rates of fire, and emplacement or displacement times for the
Type 96 and Type 99—this reference avoids extrapolation, explicitly attributing secondary
defense database figures as "Reported" and recording absent metrics as "Not stated."
Gap-Filling Reference Entries

Liaison and Fire Support Integration

The JSDF Joint Operations Command and Permanent Joint Operational C2

Historically, joint fire support coordination across Japan's Ground, Maritime, and Air
Self-Defense Forces (GSDF, MSDF, and ASDF) suffered from structural friction because the
Self-Defense Forces lacked a permanent operational headquarters. Prior to 2025, joint
contingencies required standing up ad-hoc Joint Task Forces, a model that the MOD
acknowledged impeded rapid force build-up and continuous cross-domain preparation. At the
alliance level, the Chief of Staff, Joint Staff had to split attention between strategic
military-to-military coordination with the Chairman of the U.S. Joint Chiefs of Staff and
operational theater coordination with the Commander of U.S. Indo-Pacific Command.
To close this gap, Japan formally established the JSDF Joint Operations Command (JJOC) at
Ichigaya in March 2025 under the Commander of the Joint Operations Command. Under the
National Defense Strategy and Defense Buildup Program, JJOC functions as a permanent
operational unit exercising unified, nationwide command over GSDF, MSDF, and ASDF forces
across the continuum from peacetime gray-zone activity to high-intensity contingencies. The
Joint Staff remains a staff organization advising the Minister of Defense and conveying
ministerial orders to JJOC, while JJOC executes day-to-day cross-domain synchronization and
direct operational coordination with U.S. forces—advancing alongside Washington's initiative to
upgrade U.S. Forces Japan toward a joint force headquarters. Operationally, centralizing joint
command under JJOC establishes the standing authority required to deconflict Japan's
expanding multi-domain strike inventory—including land-based Type 25 SSM and HVGP units,
maritime Tomahawk and ship-launched SSM salvos, and air-launched stand-off munitions.
While official MOD white papers confirm JJOC's permanent unified command and cross-domain
mandate, detailed joint fires allocation procedures remain unpublished in open sources.

Regional Army Artillery Centralization and Integrated Targeting Cells

Below the joint operational level, the JGSDF has fundamentally restructured tactical field
artillery liaison and fire direction across Honshu and Kyushu. Between 2018 and 2024, the
JGSDF progressively abolished organic 155mm FH-70 field artillery regiments and battalions
inside maneuver divisions and brigades, consolidating tube artillery into Regional Army Artillery
Regiments directly subordinate to Regional Army headquarters. Confirmed reorganizations
include the 2018 conversion of the 4th Artillery Regiment into the 4th Battalion of the Western
Army Artillery Regiment, the activation of the Eastern Army Artillery Regiment at Camp Kita-Fuji,
the consolidation of the Northeastern Army Artillery Regiment, and the March 2024
reorganization of the 10th Artillery Regiment into the 2nd Battalion of the Middle Army Artillery
Regiment. By contrast, the Northern Army in Hokkaido retains organic mechanized divisional
and brigade artillery units equipped with tracked Type 99 howitzers, including the 2nd and 7th
Artillery Regiments and the 11th Artillery Unit.
Centralizing cannon artillery at the Regional Army echelon elevates fire support planning above
tactical maneuver brigades, placing greater weight on digital liaison, forward observation, and
cross-domain targeting cells. Within the Northern Army and 1st Artillery Brigade framework, the
JGSDF moved to fuse Regional Army Headquarters' Fire Support Coordination Centers and
Electronic Warfare Coordination Centers into an "Integrated Targeting" cell designed to couple
intelligence, electromagnetic operations, and lethal fires in cross-domain combat.
Simultaneously, bilateral training has expanded tactical fire-support liaison beyond specialized
amphibious Joint Terminal Attack Controllers: during Orient Shield 25, a U.S. 3d Marine Division
fire support officer conducted a Joint Fires Observer (JFO) class alongside JGSDF personnel,
reinforcing the sensor-to-shooter interface required when maneuver formations no longer own
organic howitzer battalions.

Loitering Munitions and Armed Drones

SHIELD: Synchronized, Hybrid, Integrated and Enhanced Littoral Defense

Japan's unmanned strike posture shifted from experimental procurement to theater-level
architectural integration in the FY2026 defense budget through the unveiling of SHIELD
(Synchronized, Hybrid, Integrated and Enhanced Littoral Defense). Conceived as an
asymmetrical defense architecture for Japan's littoral approaches, SHIELD pairs manned
formations with large quantities of relatively inexpensive unmanned aerial, surface, and
underwater vehicles (UAVs, USVs, and UUVs) to counter costly adversary manned invasion
platforms while minimizing human casualties.
The MOD allocated approximately ¥100.1 billion in FY2026 specifically to acquire unmanned
assets for SHIELD, targeting operational establishment in FY2027. This commitment sits inside
a broader ¥277.3 billion FY2026 allocation for Unmanned Defense Capabilities—one of the
Seven Pillars of the ¥43.5 trillion Defense Buildup Program, which allocates ¥1.0 trillion to
unmanned capabilities across FY2023–FY2027. Crucially, SHIELD addresses
command-and-control scalability by funding ¥2.3 billion in FY2026 for demonstration tests of a
simultaneous control system capable of coordinating multiple diverse unmanned platforms
concurrently. Rather than operating in single-service silos, SHIELD combines GSDF
ground-launched attack UAVs with MSDF Ship-Launched UAVs, Ship-Based Small UAVs, Small
Multi-Purpose USVs and UUVs, and ASDF long-range Anti-Ship UAVs into a multi-echelon
maritime denial screen.

JGSDF Tiered Attack Drones and Modular FPV Systems

Within the SHIELD framework and ground maneuver formations, the JGSDF has formalized a
four-category taxonomy of tactical strike and reconnaissance drones that extends organic
precision fires down to small-unit and coastal-defense levels:
  ●    Modular UAV (GSDF): A First-Person View (FPV) unmanned system engineered for
       short-range intelligence collection and tactical situational awareness.
  ●    Small Attack UAV I (小型攻撃用UAV I型, GSDF): A short-range attack UAV optimized to
       strike enemy vehicles and tactical land targets. According to a public-sector procurement
       tracker (Edamame Japan), the MOD awarded a ¥3.7 billion contract on March 26, 2026
       to Marubeni Aerospace for Small Attack UAV Type I.
  ●    Small Attack UAV II (GSDF): A medium-range attack UAV configured to strike
       approaching naval vessels.
  ●    Small Attack UAV III (GSDF): A long-range maritime loitering munition designed to loiter
       over distant seas, detect and identify hostile naval assets, and execute terminal strikes.
This tiered architecture follows the March 2025 completion of deliveries for earlier close-range
multipurpose UAVs and mid-range enhanced-function UAVs, as well as July 2025
demonstration trials of small shore-to-ship transport UAVs. Analysis by Taiwan's Research
Institute for Democracy, Society and Emerging Technology (DSET) notes that the MOD plans to
continue procuring Small Attack UAV Types I, II, and III under the FY2027 defense budget. For
artillery planners, these unmanned tiers fill the lethal gap between organic 81mm/120mm
mortars and heavy 155mm or SSM batteries, delivering persistent sensor-shooter coverage in
archipelagic terrain where conventional fires face radar masking and ammunition resupply
constraints.

Target Acquisition

Organic Tactical Sensors: Counter-Mortar Radar, Meteorological Systems, and
Tactical ISR UAVs
While the JTPS-P16 handles long-range counter-battery acquisition against hostile tube and
rocket artillery, tactical JGSDF artillery regiments rely on an integrated suite of short-range,
environmental, and aerial organic sensors to close local fire missions. In mechanized formations
such as the 7th Artillery Regiment, the JTPS-P16 operates alongside the JMPQ-P13
Counter-Mortar Radar and the JMMQ-M5 Meteorological Measurement System. Introduced
in FY1986 as the Toshiba-built replacement for the Type 72 (JAN/MPQ-N1) radar, the
JMPQ-P13 is an X-band radar employing a B-scope display designed specifically to locate
short-range, low-muzzle-velocity hostile mortars. Weighing approximately 2,900 kg (approx. 5.3
m × 2.5 m × 3.5 m) and powered by an AC 115V / 10 kVA turbine generator, it provides
all-weather simultaneous multi-target location, automatic computation, and remote operation.
For aerial artillery observation and beyond-line-of-sight reconnaissance, the JGSDF historically
fielded the Remote-Controlled Unmanned Observation System (FFRS), developed by the
Defense Agency's Technical Research and Development Institute and built by Fuji Heavy
Industries, pairing a pre-programmed UAV with ground control, tracking, launch, and support
vehicles over wireless and wired datalinks. More recently, mid-range aerial ISR has shifted to
the ScanEagle / ScanEagle 2 fleet ("mid-range UAV"), operated by Regional Army and
divisional intelligence units such as the Northeastern Army Intelligence Collection Unit and the
6th Intelligence Unit. GSDF leadership confirms that ScanEagle units support maritime and land
intelligence collection, proving particularly valuable in severe weather that grounds manned
reconnaissance helicopters. At the lowest tactical echelon, defense press reporting (Militarnyi)
indicates Japan's acquisition of semi-active laser-guided Vulcano 155mm munitions is paired
with man-portable laser-designating drones (reported maximum flight range no more than 10
km, transportable in a soldier's bag) capable of terminally designating targets for both 155mm
guided shells and ASDF F-2 L-JDAM munitions.

Cross-Domain Stand-Off Target Acquisition: Space Constellations, EW Targeting,
and Allied ISR Dependencies

Fielding ground-based stand-off weapons with reported ranges near 1,000 km (Type 25 SSM
and Type 25 HVGP) creates an acute sensor-shooter mismatch: no organic JGSDF ground
radar or tactical drone can detect, track, and update moving maritime or relocatable land targets
at theater distances. Consequently, Defense of Japan 2026 details a cross-domain target
acquisition build-up anchored in orbital and electromagnetic sensors. Starting in FY2025, the
MOD initiated an indigenous image satellite constellation specifically to provide target
detection and tracking for stand-off defense, complemented by R&D on space-based infrared
sensors (leveraging JAXA's HTV-X platform) for early detection and tracking of adversary
hypersonic glide vehicles. Resilient targeting data transport is being reinforced through X-band
"Kirameki" defense communications satellites, planned successor and tactical AI satellites,
inter-satellite optical links, commercial services such as Starlink, and participation in the U.S.-led
Protected Anti-jam Tactical SATCOM (PATS) framework.
At the operational-tactical seam, the JGSDF is operationalizing passive electromagnetic
emissions as an active fires sensor. During the August 16–25, 2026 Type 88 SSM Annual Firing
Exercise at Shizunai, the 3rd and 8th Surface-to-Ship Missile Regiments trained directly
alongside electronic warfare units equipped with the Network Electronic Warfare System
(NEWS). NEWS detachments collected and analyzed radio-wave intelligence to feed situational
awareness directly into missile regiment command posts—testing formal intelligence-EW-fires
staff coordination, though actual RF emissions were withheld and scenarios simulated to avoid
disrupting local civilian spectrum use. Despite these sovereign investments, commentary in Asia
Times assesses that Japan's counterstrike architecture currently remains reliant on U.S.
space-based ISR and target-processing feeds via Combined Joint All-Domain Command and
Control (CJADC2) channels while Tokyo develops domestic satellite capacity and explores
supplementary ISR partnerships with the United Kingdom, Australia, and Finland.

Counter-Fire and Force Protection

Layered Counter-UAS, Directed Energy R&D, and Radar-Site Defense Drones

Traditional gun-area air defense built around the Type 11 Short-Range Surface-to-Air Missile
(SHORAD) faces acute cost-exchange and saturation pressures against small reconnaissance
drones and loitering munitions. To protect artillery firing positions, missile launchers, and radar
sites, the MOD is layering electronic, directed-energy, and counter-drone kinetic measures
beneath conventional SAMs. At the unit level, JGSDF garrisons and field units already sustain
fielded Small UAV Countermeasure Equipment (小型無人機対処器材)—mobile electronic
C-UAS systems subject to periodic RF transmission/reception calibration and manufacturer
control-software updates.
To defeat drone swarms without exhausting expensive missile magazines, the Defense Buildup
Program allocates ¥0.07 trillion (¥70 billion) for research into High-Power Laser and
High-Power Microwave (HPM) directed-energy technologies. Building on an earlier
approximately ¥1.1 billion HPM technology study (FY2014–FY2020) that conducted
anechoic-chamber validation, the MOD and the U.S. Department of Defense signed a bilateral
Project Arrangement in July 2024 to conduct joint research on HPM systems. Complementing
directed energy, the FY2026 budget explicitly introduces an active unmanned interceptor layer:
ASDF Radar Site Defense UAVs, designed to defend high-value radar installations against
hostile UAVs and aerial threats.

Networked Command-Post Dispersion and Mobility-Based Survivability

Active air defense cannot guarantee gun-area survival under persistent orbital ISR and
adversary counter-fire; passive signature reduction and physical dispersion are equally decisive.
A concrete doctrinal shift appeared in the August 2026 Type 88 SSM Annual Firing Exercise at
Shizunai, where the GSDF Chief of Staff highlighted a major upgrade over prior-year iterations:
physically separating the missile regiment headquarters (command post) from the firing
area and connecting them via tactical networks across dispersed sites. Decoupling
high-emitting C2 headquarters from launch platoons reduces the vulnerability of losing both
command staff and launchers to a single adversary direction-finding or counter-battery strike.
This dispersion complements platform-level survivability choices across JGSDF fires units. In
Northern Army mechanized formations, the tracked Type 99 SPH provides high mobility and
armor protection designed for resilient fires operations (official ATLA assessment; aluminum
alloy armor Reported), while the tracked Type 96 MSP provides steel armor against small-arms
fire and shell splinters with rapid post-firing relocation (Reported). Across Honshu and Kyushu,
transitioning regional artillery battalions from towed FH-70 howitzers to wheeled Type 19
155mm self-propelled howitzers directly compresses shoot-and-scoot vulnerability windows. At
the strategic-doctrinal level, Japan's National Institute for Defense Studies (NIDS) emphasizes
that surviving modern deep-strike campaigns requires systematic decentralized deployment of
military assets paired with unmanned platforms.
Operational and Doctrinal Lessons Learned

Lessons from the War in Ukraine: Attrition, Unmanned-Artillery
Complexes, and Industrial Surge

Defense of Japan 2026 devotes explicit doctrinal analysis to Russia's invasion of Ukraine,
identifying three primary fires-related lessons that directly shape Japanese force design. First,
the MOD highlights the battlefield synergy of complex combined attacks in which massive
numbers of inexpensive, diverse unmanned assets operate alongside conventional artillery
shells and missiles to strike expensive military platforms and critical infrastructure. Second, the
white paper underscores an unprecedented compression of tactical and technical update
cycles, driven by AI-assisted decision-making, networked sensors, and multi-domain integration
across space, cyber, and the electromagnetic spectrum. Third, Ukraine has revalidated the
harsh logistics of protracted attritional warfare, where both sides expend artillery ammunition
and major end-items at rates that strain peacetime reserves and defense supply chains.
These observations have translated into tangible Japanese policy and procurement reforms
rather than abstract commentary. In direct response to the unmanned-artillery threat—and
mindful of MOD and NIDS assessments that China is studying the same Ukraine lessons
regarding massed unmanned assets, AI, and asymmetric operations—Tokyo launched SHIELD
(approx. ¥100.1 billion in FY2026) and instituted a Fast-Track Procurement program to
accelerate adoption of commercial startup technologies. On the sustainment front, NIDS
researcher Dr. Watanabe emphasized that Ukraine demonstrates peacetime stockpiles alone
are insufficient without rapid surge production capacity, supply-chain visibility, and diversified
sourcing. Accordingly, the MOD paired its ¥2.0 trillion dedicated FY2023–FY2027 ammunition
allocation (¥5.0 trillion including related areas) with the Act on Enhancing Defense Production
and Technology Bases to stabilize parts supply and expand domestic ammunition
manufacturing capacity.

Lessons from Multilateral Exercises and Domestic Live-Fire
Adaptation

Complementing lessons observed abroad, the JGSDF has extracted actionable procedural
lessons from intensifying bilateral and trilateral field training exercises with the United States
and Australia. During Resolute Dragon 21—then the largest bilateral USMC–JGSDF field
exercise in Japan—artillery and fire support coordination officers from 4th Marine Regiment and
the JGSDF practiced integrated kill chains across HIMARS and Japanese SSMs, jointly building
fire plans, sharing targeting data, and selecting engagement platforms across national lines.
Subsequent iterations (Resolute Dragon 23 and 24 with III Marine Expeditionary Force and the
JGSDF Western Army, including operations at Camp Yonaguni) deepened expeditionary fires
coordination across the Southwestern Islands. Trilateral fires interoperability accelerated
through Yama Sakura 85 (the first trilateral U.S.–Japan–Australia command post exercise in
December 2023) and Orient Shield 26 in Hokkaido (September 2026), which marked the
second consecutive year of Australian Army participation—incorporating the Australian 10th
Fires Brigade alongside U.S. Army Pacific and JGSDF formations to validate human, technical,
and procedural multi-domain interoperability.
Domestically, operational exercises exposed a critical readiness bottleneck: Japan's geographic
density and airspace/maritime safety constraints historically forced JGSDF long-range missile
and extended-range artillery units to rely on overseas ranges—such as U.S. test ranges for the
2025 Type 25 SSM launch campaign and reported UK trials for Type 19 Vulcano firings.
Addressing this constraint in August 2026, the GSDF Chief of Staff emphasized that conducting
regiment-scale Type 88 SSM live-fire drills domestically at the Shizunai Anti-Aircraft Firing
Range—incorporating networked command-post dispersion and NEWS EW coordination—and
developing future domestic firing infrastructure such as Minamitorishima are essential for
securing regular, realistic live-fire proficiency across Japan's expanding long-range fires force.

Three source discrepancies warrant attention when briefing from open-source material on these
systems. First, regarding the Type 99 engine cooling method, the secondary
WeaponSystems.net database describes the Mitsubishi 6SY31WA as air-cooled, whereas the
official JGSDF 2nd Artillery Regiment specification explicitly confirms a water-cooled 4-cycle
6-cylinder diesel; the official JGSDF specification governs. Second, an older archived Forecast
International report lists the Type 99 with a 39-calibre barrel and 310 hp engine by conflating
early development concepts with the FH-70 and Type 89 IFV; current defense references
consistently confirm the production 52-calibre ordnance and 600 hp powerplant. Third, for the
Type 96 MSP, the reported 6–10 rpm rate of fire reflects confined interior operation and manual
hand-laying without an electronic fire control system, explaining why it trails the 15–20 rpm
official maximum of the dismounted towed 120mm RT.
