Artillery and Fire Support Update:
Southeast Asia and Oceania (1 May to 24
November 2026)

This open-source update records verified changes in artillery and fire support from 1 May 2026
to 24 November 2026 for four armies: Australia, Indonesia, Malaysia, and Singapore. Across the
four forces, 33 new or corrective items were verified after excluding events already recorded in
the baseline reference. Australia accounted for 15 items, led by accelerated M142 HIMARS
deliveries, initial AS9 Huntsman field deployment, and a second domestic 155mm shell forging
contract. Indonesia recorded 3 items, centred on a parliamentary briefing confirming new field
and air-defence artillery battalions within a 750-battalion expansion plan and a framework export
deal for Bayraktar Kizilelma unmanned combat aircraft. Malaysia recorded 8 items, including
fire-control standardisation across 29 towed G5 155mm howitzers and training infrastructure
actions at its artillery school. Singapore recorded 7 items, including official confirmation that its
Truck Mounted Gun will replace both legacy 155mm howitzers, United States Foreign Military
Sale approval for HIMARS fire-control upgrades, and bilateral rocket live firing in the United
States.
Official government, parliamentary, audit, and armed forces disclosures provide the primary
evidence base. Items resting on defence press, manufacturer statements, or single social-media
posts begin with "Reported:" and are weighed against official releases. Where open sources
disagree on quantities, course lengths, or exercise dates, each figure is stated alongside its
source.
Australia (1 May 2026 to 24 November 2026)

1. Equipment

 ● 7 October 2026: The Australian Army received its second tranche of M142 HIMARS
 launchers from the United States two years ahead of schedule, one year after the first
 tranche arrived. The government did not publish the number of launchers in this batch,
 but confirmed a peak fleet of up to 90 HIMARS units backed by up to A37 billion over ten
 years for targeting and long-range strike and up to A36 billion for the Guided Weapons
 and Explosive Ordnance (GWEO) Enterprise. The release also confirmed that a second
 domestic missile manufacturing factory will open near Newcastle, New South Wales, in
 2027 alongside the existing South Australia facility. Source: "Albanese Government
 continues to strengthen Army's long-range strike capability | Defence Ministers",
 Australian Government, Defence Ministers (Department of Defence), 7 October 2026,
 https://www.minister.defence.gov.au/media-releases/2026-10-07/albanese-government-co
 ntinues-strengthen-armys-long-range-strike-capability.
 ● Reported: Autumn 2026 (published prior to 16 October 2026): Electro Optic Systems
 (EOS) supplied small numbers of R400 Slinger remote weapon stations to the Australian
 Army for Project LAND 156 counter-drone trials, mounted on Hawkei protected mobility
 vehicles. Source: "Land Forces 2026: Australia decides", Global Defence Technology
 (NRI Digital), Autumn 2026,
 https://defence.nridigital.com/global_defence_technology_aut26/land_forces_2026_austra
 lia_decides.

 Rheinmetall NIOA Munitions to build a second domestic large-calibre artillery forging
 capability at Maryborough, Queensland. The contractor-owned, contractor-operated forge
 is scheduled to begin operating by the end of 2028, initially producing 15,000 155mm
 M795 projectiles per year for the M777A2 and AS9 Huntsman howitzers, with capacity to
 scale higher. Source: "Australia fires up second artillery forging capability | Defence
 Ministers", Australian Government, Defence Ministers (Department of Defence), 8 June
 2026,
 https://www.minister.defence.gov.au/media-releases/2026-06-08/australia-fires-up-second

 Australian Artillery, fired the first Australian-built AS9 Huntsman 155mm self-propelled

3. Doctrine, training and schools

 contribution fact sheet, formalising its strategy of denial in the littoral environment. The
 document integrates 10th Brigade's long-range fires (HIMARS, PrSM, and GMLRS) and
 ground-based air defence (NASAMS) with protected mobile close fires (AS9 Huntsman)
 and domestically produced landing craft. Source: "The Australian Army Contribution to the
 National Defence Strategy / 2026", Australian Army, July 2026,

 Army's inaugural AS9 Huntsman operator course for 30 gunners from 4th Regiment,
 Royal Australian Artillery, covering tracked vehicle driving and automated gun laying.
 Sources disagree on course duration: the Australian Department of Defence (4 June
 2026) and Australian Defence Magazine (4 June 2026) report a six-week course, whereas
 Janes (6 July 2026) reports a two-month operator course for drivers, loaders, gunners,
 and assistant gunners, followed by a one-month commander's course for bombardiers
 and commanders. Sources: "Australian-built howitzer sends the thunder | Defence",

 M142 HIMARS launchers overseas for the first time, alongside two M777 155mm
 howitzers and RAAF No. 4 Squadron PC-21 forward air control aircraft. Live-fire serials

5. Operations and lessons

 ● June 2026 (audit status as at June 2026): An Australian National Audit Office (ANAO)
 performance audit of the A$1.1 billion Strategic Domestic Munitions Manufacturing
 contract with Thales Australia found that critical assets at both Commonwealth-owned
 factories (Mulwala and Benalla) were assessed as below target condition as at April 2026,
 with remediation ongoing. As at June 2026, Defence had approved three remediation
 plans submitted by Thales to address performance shortfalls, though implementation
 remained at an early stage and Defence's AusTender reporting did not accurately reflect
 total work placed under the contract. Source: "Defence's Management of Contracts for the
 Supply of Munitions – Part 2", Australian National Audit Office (ANAO), 2026 (status as at
 June 2026),
 https://www.anao.gov.au/work/performance-audit/defences-management-of-contracts-for-t
 he-supply-of-munitions-part-2.
 ● 29 May 2026 (and 22 May 2026): Australian Army instructors mentoring Ukrainian
 non-commissioned officers on Operation Kudu reported absorbing battlefield lessons from
 drone-saturated combat. Training adaptations included weaving counter-drone awareness
 across all tactical drills rather than treating it as a standalone lesson, standing beside
 vertical cover such as trees to lower visual signature instead of defaulting to prone
 positions, and planning for prolonged casualty care when persistent drone observation
 prevents medical evacuation within the "golden hour". Sources: "Adapting to a
 fast-moving battlefield", Australian Department of Defence, 29 May 2026,
 https://www.defence.gov.au/news-events/news/2026-05-29/adapting-fast-moving-battlefiel
 d; "Training to lead in the chaos of modern war", Australian Department of Defence, 22
 May 2026,
 https://www.defence.gov.au/news-events/news/2026-05-22/training-lead-chaos-modern-w
 ar.
